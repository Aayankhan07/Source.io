# Introductory Ecology: Course Reader

--- Page 1 ---

This reader accompanies an introductory course in ecology, the scientific study of how organisms interact with one another and with their physical surroundings. The material moves from the smallest ecological unit treated here, the population, upward through communities, ecosystems, and biomes, and then turns to change over time and to the practical problem of conservation. Each chapter introduces core vocabulary, explains the underlying mechanisms, and offers worked examples drawn from well-studied systems. Students should read each chapter before the corresponding lecture and use the subsections as a checklist of concepts that may appear on quizzes and examinations.

Ecologists usually describe their subject as a hierarchy of levels of organization. An individual organism belongs to a population, which is a group of individuals of one species living in the same area at the same time. Populations of different species that share an area form a community. A community together with its nonliving environment, including soil, water, air, and sunlight, makes up an ecosystem. Large regions with characteristic climate and vegetation are called biomes, and the sum of all ecosystems on Earth is the biosphere.

## Chapter 1: Populations

### 1.1 Describing a Population

A population is characterized by several measurable properties. Population size, symbolized N, is simply the number of individuals present. Population density is the number of individuals per unit area or volume, such as 120 oak seedlings per hectare or 4,000 water fleas per liter of pond water. Dispersion describes the spatial pattern of individuals and comes in three classic forms. A clumped pattern, the most common in nature, occurs when individuals aggregate around patchy resources or live in social groups, as wolves in packs do. A uniform pattern, with roughly even spacing, often results from territorial behavior, such as nesting seabirds that peck at neighbors who come too close. A random pattern, where the position of one individual gives no information about the position of another, is relatively rare and appears where resources are evenly spread and individuals neither attract nor repel one another, as with some dandelions dispersed by wind.

Because counting every individual is usually impossible, ecologists estimate population size with sampling methods. Quadrat sampling involves counting organisms inside a number of square frames placed across a habitat and scaling the average up to the total area. For mobile animals, the mark-recapture method is standard. In its simplest form, known as the Lincoln-Petersen estimate, a researcher captures and marks a first sample (M), releases the animals, and later captures a second sample (C) in which R individuals are found to be marked. The population estimate is N = (M × C) / R. For example, if 50 fish are tagged, and a later catch of 40 fish contains 10 tagged ones, the estimated population is 200 fish. The method assumes that marks are not lost, that marked animals mix freely with unmarked ones, and that the population is closed to births, deaths, and migration between samples.

### 1.2 Demography and Life Tables

Demography is the study of the vital statistics that change population size: births, deaths, immigration, and emigration. A life table summarizes the survival and reproduction of a cohort, a group of individuals born at the same time, as it ages. From a life table an ecologist can draw a survivorship curve, which plots the number of survivors against age, usually on a logarithmic scale.

Three idealized survivorship curves are taught in nearly every course. A Type I curve is flat at first and then drops steeply late in life; it describes species such as humans and elephants that produce few offspring but give them substantial care, so most individuals live to old age. A Type II curve is a straight diagonal line, meaning the death rate is roughly constant across all ages; many songbirds, lizards, and small rodents approximate this pattern. A Type III curve drops sharply at the start and then flattens; it fits organisms such as oysters, many fish, and most trees, which release enormous numbers of offspring that mostly die young, while the few that survive early life can live a long time.

### 1.3 Exponential Growth

When resources are unlimited, a population grows in proportion to its current size. The per capita rate of increase, r, equals the per capita birth rate minus the per capita death rate. The exponential growth model is written dN/dt = rN. Plotted over time, exponential growth produces a J-shaped curve that becomes steeper as the population becomes larger. Bacteria in fresh culture medium, or a species introduced to an island free of predators, can show this pattern for a while. A useful rule of thumb is that the doubling time of an exponentially growing population is approximately 70 divided by the percentage growth rate; a population growing at 2 percent per year doubles in about 35 years.

### 1.4 Logistic Growth and Carrying Capacity

No population can grow exponentially forever. Space, food, water, nesting sites, and other resources eventually run short. The carrying capacity, symbolized K, is the maximum population size that a particular environment can sustain over the long term. The logistic growth model adds a braking term to the exponential equation: dN/dt = rN(K − N)/K. When N is small, the term (K − N)/K is close to 1 and growth is nearly exponential. As N approaches K, the term approaches zero and growth slows to a halt. The resulting curve is S-shaped, or sigmoid. The fastest absolute growth occurs when the population is at half of the carrying capacity, a fact that fisheries managers once used to define a maximum sustainable yield.

### 1.5 Density-Dependent and Density-Independent Factors

Factors that regulate population size fall into two broad groups. Density-dependent factors have effects that intensify as the population becomes more crowded. Competition for food, the spread of infectious disease, the accumulation of toxic wastes, and predation that focuses on the most abundant prey are all density-dependent. Density-independent factors affect a population regardless of how crowded it is; a hard frost, a flood, a drought, or a wildfire can kill the same proportion of individuals whether the population is large or small.

A famous illustration of population cycles comes from the snowshoe hare and Canada lynx, whose numbers rise and fall in a cycle of roughly 10 years, as revealed by fur-trading records spanning more than a century. Field experiments suggest that the hare cycle is driven by a combination of food limitation in winter and predation pressure, and that the lynx population tracks the hare population with a short lag.

### 1.6 Life History Strategies

A life history is the pattern of an organism's growth, reproduction, and survival over its lifetime. Ecologists have long contrasted r-selected species, which thrive in unpredictable environments, mature quickly, and produce many small offspring, with K-selected species, which live in stable environments near carrying capacity, mature slowly, and produce few large offspring that receive extensive parental care. Weedy annual plants and insects are typical r-selected examples, while whales, great apes, and large hardwood trees are typical K-selected examples. Most species fall somewhere in between, and modern ecologists treat the r and K labels as two ends of a continuum rather than strict categories.

## Chapter 2: Communities

### 2.1 Interspecific Interactions

A community is defined by the interactions among its member species. These interactions can be classified by their effect on each participant, written as plus (+), minus (−), or zero (0). Competition is a −/− interaction in which two species use the same limited resource. Predation, herbivory, and parasitism are +/− interactions in which one species benefits at the expense of another. Mutualism is a +/+ interaction that benefits both partners, as when pollinating bees collect nectar while transferring pollen between flowers. Commensalism is a +/0 interaction in which one species benefits and the other is unaffected; cattle egrets that feed on insects stirred up by grazing cattle are a common example.

### 2.2 Competition and the Niche

The ecological niche of a species is the full set of biotic and abiotic resources it uses and the conditions it tolerates. The fundamental niche is the range a species could occupy in the absence of competitors, while the realized niche is the narrower range it actually occupies when competitors are present.

The competitive exclusion principle, associated with the Russian biologist G. F. Gause, holds that two species competing for exactly the same limiting resources cannot coexist indefinitely in the same place; one will eventually outcompete and eliminate the other. Gause demonstrated this in the 1930s using two species of Paramecium grown in laboratory cultures: when grown together, Paramecium aurelia consistently drove Paramecium caudatum to local extinction.

Species can coexist, however, if they divide resources in some way, a process called resource partitioning. Ecologist Robert MacArthur showed in the 1950s that five species of warblers feeding in the same spruce trees in New England foraged in different zones of the tree, some near the trunk and some at the outer branch tips, some high and some low. Joseph Connell's classic experiments on the rocky coast of Scotland showed that one barnacle species, Chthamalus, was confined to the upper intertidal zone because a stronger competitor, Balanus, crowded it out of the lower zone; when Balanus was removed, Chthamalus spread downward into its fundamental niche.

### 2.3 Predation and Defense

Predators exert strong selection on prey, and prey have evolved a remarkable array of defenses. Cryptic coloration, or camouflage, makes prey hard to see. Aposematic coloration is bright warning coloration that advertises chemical defenses, as in poison dart frogs. In Batesian mimicry, a harmless species evolves to resemble a harmful one; the nonvenomous hawkmoth larva that resembles a small snake is an example. In Müllerian mimicry, two or more harmful species converge on a similar warning appearance, so that predators learn to avoid all of them more quickly; several species of stinging bees and wasps share yellow and black banding for this reason.

### 2.4 Keystone Species and Trophic Cascades

Some species have an influence on community structure far out of proportion to their abundance. These are called keystone species. The concept comes from Robert Paine's experiments in the 1960s on the Pacific coast of Washington State. When Paine removed the predatory sea star Pisaster ochraceus from experimental plots, mussels took over the rocks and the number of species in the plots fell from about 15 to fewer than 10 within a few years. The sea star, by eating mussels, had been keeping space open for many other species.

Sea otters in the North Pacific offer another example. Otters eat sea urchins, which graze on kelp. Where otters were hunted out, urchin populations exploded and turned kelp forests into barren rocky areas called urchin barrens. This chain of effects, in which a top predator indirectly controls the abundance of plants by controlling herbivores, is called a trophic cascade.

## Chapter 3: Energy Flow in Ecosystems

### 3.1 Producers, Consumers, and Decomposers

Every ecosystem runs on a flow of energy that enters, is transformed, and eventually leaves as heat. Nearly all of that energy originates as sunlight. Primary producers, also called autotrophs, capture light energy through photosynthesis and store it in chemical bonds; on land these are mainly plants, and in the oceans they are mainly phytoplankton. A small number of ecosystems, such as those around deep-sea hydrothermal vents, are powered instead by chemosynthetic bacteria that obtain energy by oxidizing inorganic compounds like hydrogen sulfide.

Consumers, or heterotrophs, obtain energy by eating other organisms. Primary consumers are herbivores that eat producers. Secondary consumers eat primary consumers, and tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers. Detritivores and decomposers, including earthworms, fungi, and bacteria, feed on dead organic matter, called detritus, and are essential for releasing nutrients back into the ecosystem. Each feeding step is called a trophic level. A simple linear sequence of feeding relationships is a food chain; the interconnected network of many food chains in a real ecosystem is a food web.

### 3.2 Primary Production

Gross primary production (GPP) is the total amount of energy that producers capture by photosynthesis in a given area over a given time. Producers use a portion of that energy for their own cellular respiration. What remains, the energy stored in new plant biomass, is net primary production (NPP), so NPP = GPP − respiration by producers. NPP is the energy actually available to consumers and is typically expressed in grams of dry mass or kilocalories per square meter per year.

Global NPP varies enormously by ecosystem. Tropical rain forests and estuaries are among the most productive ecosystems per unit area, while deserts and the open ocean are among the least productive. Because the open ocean covers such a vast area, however, it contributes roughly half of the planet's total net primary production. On land, production is typically limited by temperature and moisture; in aquatic systems, it is usually limited by light and by nutrients, especially nitrogen and phosphorus. An experiment off the coast of Long Island showed that adding nitrogen, but not phosphorus, increased phytoplankton growth, identifying nitrogen as the limiting nutrient in those waters.

### 3.3 Ecological Efficiency and the Ten Percent Rule

When a herbivore eats a plant, most of the energy in that plant never becomes herbivore tissue. Some plant material is not eaten at all, some is eaten but passes through the gut undigested, and much of the energy that is absorbed is spent on respiration and lost as heat. The percentage of energy transferred from one trophic level to the next is called trophic efficiency. It typically ranges from about 5 to 20 percent, and a convenient approximation known as the ten percent rule states that roughly 10 percent of the energy at one trophic level is passed to the next.

The consequences of this rule are profound. If producers in a grassland store 10,000 kilocalories per square meter per year, herbivores might capture about 1,000, primary carnivores about 100, and secondary carnivores only about 10. This steep loss explains why food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels and why top predators such as tigers and eagles are relatively rare.

### 3.4 Ecological Pyramids

Ecologists summarize trophic structure with pyramids. A pyramid of energy, which shows the energy flowing through each level per unit time, always narrows toward the top because of the energy losses described above. A pyramid of biomass shows the dry mass of organisms at each level at one moment. Most biomass pyramids also narrow toward the top, but some aquatic systems have an inverted biomass pyramid, in which the standing mass of zooplankton exceeds the mass of phytoplankton. This is possible because phytoplankton reproduce so rapidly that a small standing stock can support a larger mass of grazers; the turnover time of the producers is very short. A pyramid of numbers counts individuals at each level and can also be inverted, as when a single large tree supports thousands of insects.

### 3.5 Biological Magnification

Some toxic substances, especially those that are fat-soluble and not easily broken down, become more concentrated at each step up a food chain. This process is called biological magnification or biomagnification. The insecticide DDT is the classic case. In the mid-twentieth century, DDT concentrations in the tissues of fish-eating birds such as ospreys, brown pelicans, and bald eagles were millions of times higher than in the surrounding water. DDT interfered with calcium deposition in eggshells, causing the shells to become thin and break during incubation, and populations of these birds declined sharply. Rachel Carson's 1962 book Silent Spring drew public attention to the problem, and the United States banned most uses of DDT in 1972, after which many affected bird populations recovered.

## Chapter 4: Nutrient Cycles

### 4.1 Matter Cycles While Energy Flows

A fundamental principle of ecosystem ecology is that energy flows through ecosystems in one direction, entering as light and leaving as heat, whereas chemical elements cycle repeatedly between living organisms and the nonliving environment. These movements are called biogeochemical cycles because they involve biological, geological, and chemical processes. Each cycle can be described in terms of reservoirs, where an element is stored, and fluxes, the rates at which it moves between reservoirs.

### 4.2 The Water Cycle

The water, or hydrologic, cycle is driven mainly by solar energy and gravity. The oceans hold about 97 percent of Earth's water, while most fresh water is locked in ice caps and glaciers. Water moves to the atmosphere through evaporation from oceans and lakes and through transpiration, the loss of water vapor from plant leaves; together these processes are often called evapotranspiration. Water returns to the surface as precipitation and then flows over land as runoff or soaks into the ground to recharge groundwater stored in aquifers.

### 4.3 The Carbon Cycle

Carbon is the backbone of all organic molecules. Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and incorporates the carbon into sugars, while cellular respiration by producers, consumers, and decomposers returns it to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide. The largest reservoirs of carbon are sedimentary rocks such as limestone, followed by the deep ocean, where carbon is dissolved mainly as bicarbonate. Fossil fuels, including coal, oil, and natural gas, formed over millions of years from buried organic matter that was never fully decomposed.

Human activity has altered the carbon cycle dramatically. Burning fossil fuels and clearing forests release carbon far faster than natural processes can remove it. Atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration was about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution and passed 420 parts per million in the early 2020s, as recorded at the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii since 1958. The ocean absorbs a large share of the added carbon dioxide, but this forms carbonic acid and lowers seawater pH, a process called ocean acidification that makes it harder for corals, oysters, and some plankton to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons.

### 4.4 The Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen makes up about 78 percent of the atmosphere, yet most organisms cannot use nitrogen gas (N2) directly because its triple bond is very hard to break. The nitrogen cycle therefore depends on several groups of microorganisms. Nitrogen fixation converts N2 into ammonia (NH3) or ammonium (NH4+). It is performed by certain free-living bacteria, by cyanobacteria, and by Rhizobium bacteria that live in root nodules of legumes such as peas, beans, clover, and alfalfa; a small amount is also fixed by lightning. Nitrification is a two-step process in which soil bacteria oxidize ammonium first to nitrite (NO2−) and then to nitrate (NO3−), the form most readily absorbed by plant roots. Assimilation is the uptake of ammonium and nitrate by plants, which build them into proteins and nucleic acids. Ammonification is the decomposition of organic nitrogen back into ammonium by decomposers. Finally, denitrification, carried out by bacteria in waterlogged, low-oxygen soils, converts nitrate back into nitrogen gas, which returns to the atmosphere.

Since the early twentieth century, the industrial Haber-Bosch process has allowed people to fix atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia for fertilizer on a massive scale. Human activities now fix roughly as much nitrogen each year as all natural terrestrial processes combined.

### 4.5 The Phosphorus Cycle

Phosphorus is required for DNA, RNA, ATP, and the phospholipids in cell membranes, and it is a major component of bones and teeth. Unlike carbon and nitrogen, phosphorus has no significant gaseous phase, so its cycle is largely confined to rocks, soil, water, and organisms. Weathering of phosphate-bearing rocks slowly releases phosphate ions into soil, where plants absorb them. Phosphate eventually washes into the oceans, where it settles into sediments that may be uplifted into new rock only over geological time spans. Because phosphate is released so slowly, it is often a limiting nutrient in freshwater lakes and in many tropical soils.

### 4.6 Eutrophication

When excess nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizer runoff, sewage, or animal waste enter lakes and coastal waters, they can trigger explosive growth of algae, a process called eutrophication. When the algae die, bacteria decompose them and consume large amounts of dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic conditions in which fish and other animals suffocate. The Gulf of Mexico develops a seasonal hypoxic dead zone each summer near the mouth of the Mississippi River, fed by nutrients washed from farmland across the river's huge drainage basin; in some years it has covered more than 15,000 square kilometers. Whole-lake experiments in the Experimental Lakes Area of northwestern Ontario during the 1970s showed that phosphorus was the key driver of algal blooms in freshwater lakes, findings that led many governments to restrict phosphates in laundry detergents.

## Chapter 5: Biomes

### 5.1 What Determines a Biome

A biome is a major type of ecological community distinguished by its dominant vegetation on land, or by its physical characteristics in water, and spread across large geographic areas. The distribution of terrestrial biomes is controlled mainly by climate, especially annual mean temperature and annual precipitation, and by how those values vary through the seasons. A climograph plots these two variables against each other and shows that each biome occupies a characteristic region of temperature-precipitation space. Global patterns of air circulation explain much of this distribution. Warm, moist air rises near the equator, cools, and drops heavy rain, producing tropical forests; the now-dry air descends around 30 degrees north and south latitude, which is why many of the world's great deserts, including the Sahara, lie near those latitudes.

### 5.2 Tropical Rain Forest

Tropical rain forests occur near the equator, where temperatures stay between about 25 and 29 degrees Celsius all year and annual rainfall commonly exceeds 200 centimeters. They contain more species of plants and animals than any other terrestrial biome. The vegetation is arranged in distinct vertical layers: emergent trees, a dense closed canopy, a shaded understory, and a dim forest floor. Surprisingly, the soils are often thin and nutrient-poor, because heavy rains leach minerals away and because most nutrients are held in living biomass and recycled quickly by decomposers. This is why rain forest land cleared for agriculture often loses fertility within a few years.

### 5.3 Desert

Deserts receive less than about 30 centimeters of precipitation per year. Some are hot, like the Sonoran Desert of North America, while others, such as the Gobi of central Asia, are cold for much of the year. Desert plants show adaptations for conserving water. Cacti and other succulents store water in fleshy stems, and many desert plants use CAM photosynthesis, opening their stomata only at night to reduce water loss.

### 5.4 Grasslands and Savanna

Temperate grasslands, such as the North American prairie, the Eurasian steppe, and the South American pampas, have cold winters, hot summers, and moderate precipitation of roughly 30 to 100 centimeters per year. Periodic drought, fire, and grazing by large mammals prevent trees from becoming established. Grassland soils are among the deepest and most fertile in the world, which is why most temperate grasslands have been converted to cropland. Savannas are tropical grasslands with scattered trees, such as the acacia savannas of East Africa, and they experience a pronounced dry season. Savannas support large herds of grazing mammals, including wildebeest, zebras, and gazelles, along with predators such as lions and cheetahs.

### 5.5 Temperate Deciduous Forest

Temperate deciduous forests grow in regions with warm summers, cold winters, and fairly even precipitation of about 70 to 200 centimeters per year, as in eastern North America, much of Europe, and eastern Asia. Broadleaf trees such as oaks, maples, beeches, and hickories drop their leaves in autumn, an adaptation that reduces water loss and frost damage during winter. The yearly leaf fall produces a rich layer of litter and relatively fertile soil.

### 5.6 Taiga and Tundra

The taiga, also called the boreal forest or northern coniferous forest, is the largest terrestrial biome on Earth, forming a broad belt across northern North America, Scandinavia, and Russia. Winters are long and very cold, and the growing season is short. The dominant trees are conifers such as spruce, fir, and pine, whose needle-shaped leaves and conical shape help them shed snow and conserve water. Soils are acidic and decompose slowly, so thick layers of organic matter accumulate.

North of the taiga lies the arctic tundra, a treeless biome with extremely cold winters and a growing season that may last only 50 to 60 days. Annual precipitation is low, often under 25 centimeters, which makes the tundra nearly as dry as a desert. The defining feature of the tundra is permafrost, a layer of soil that remains frozen year-round and prevents roots from penetrating deeply. Vegetation consists of low shrubs, grasses, sedges, mosses, and lichens. As the climate warms, thawing permafrost is releasing stored carbon as carbon dioxide and methane, which may further accelerate warming.

### 5.7 Aquatic Biomes

Aquatic biomes cover about three quarters of Earth's surface and are divided into freshwater and marine types based on salt concentration. Freshwater biomes include lakes, ponds, rivers, streams, and wetlands, and typically have salt concentrations below 1 percent. Marine biomes have an average salinity of about 3.5 percent.

The ocean is divided into the intertidal zone, where land meets sea and organisms are alternately submerged and exposed; the neritic zone over the continental shelf; and the vast oceanic zone beyond. The photic zone is the upper layer, down to about 200 meters, where enough light penetrates for photosynthesis; below it lies the aphotic zone. Coral reefs, built by colonies of coral animals that host photosynthetic algae called zooxanthellae, form in warm, shallow, clear tropical waters and are among the most diverse ecosystems on the planet. Estuaries, where rivers meet the sea and fresh water mixes with salt water, serve as nurseries for many commercially important fish and shellfish.

## Chapter 6: Ecological Succession

### 6.1 Disturbance and Change

Communities are not static. Disturbances such as fires, storms, floods, landslides, volcanic eruptions, and human land clearing remove organisms and alter resource availability. Ecologists characterize disturbance regimes by their frequency, intensity, and size. The intermediate disturbance hypothesis proposes that species diversity is often highest at moderate levels of disturbance. Where disturbance is rare, a few competitively dominant species exclude others; where it is severe and frequent, only a few hardy, fast-colonizing species can persist. Moderate disturbance opens space for colonists without wiping out the community entirely, allowing both early- and late-arriving species to coexist.

### 6.2 Primary Succession

Ecological succession is the gradual, somewhat predictable change in species composition of a community over time following a disturbance. Primary succession begins in an area essentially devoid of life where no soil has yet formed, such as newly cooled lava, rock exposed by a retreating glacier, or a new volcanic island. The first organisms to arrive, known as pioneer species, are often lichens and mosses, together with prokaryotes. Lichens secrete acids that help break down rock, and when pioneers die their remains add organic matter, gradually building a thin soil. Over decades to centuries, grasses, shrubs, and eventually trees can establish.

A carefully studied example is Glacier Bay in southeastern Alaska, where glaciers have retreated roughly 100 kilometers since the late 1700s. Researchers can walk from recently exposed ground near the ice to areas exposed for more than two centuries, observing a sequence from a pioneer stage of mosses, lichens, and fireweed, through a stage dominated by mat-forming Dryas, then a stage of alder shrubs, and finally spruce and hemlock forest. Alder hosts nitrogen-fixing bacteria in its roots and greatly increases soil nitrogen, which makes it easier for spruce to grow later, a clear case of earlier species changing conditions for later ones.

### 6.3 Secondary Succession

Secondary succession occurs where a disturbance has removed the existing community but left the soil intact. It follows events such as forest fires, hurricanes, or the abandonment of farmland. Because soil, seeds, and roots are already present, secondary succession proceeds much faster than primary succession. A classic sequence in the eastern United States occurs on abandoned farm fields: annual weeds dominate in the first year or two, followed by perennial grasses and herbs, then shrubs, then fast-growing pines, and eventually, after roughly 100 to 150 years, a hardwood forest of oaks and hickories.

### 6.4 Mechanisms of Succession

Ecologists Joseph Connell and Ralph Slatyer proposed in 1977 three models describing how early species influence later ones. In the facilitation model, early species modify the environment in ways that make it more suitable for later species, as the nitrogen-fixing alders of Glacier Bay do. In the inhibition model, early species make conditions less suitable for later arrivals, and replacement occurs only when the early occupants are damaged or die. In the tolerance model, early species neither help nor hinder later ones; later species simply grow more slowly but tolerate lower resource levels, eventually outcompeting the pioneers. Real successional sequences usually involve a mixture of all three mechanisms.

### 6.5 Climax Communities

Early ecologists, especially Frederic Clements in the early twentieth century, viewed succession as an orderly progression toward a stable endpoint called the climax community, determined primarily by regional climate. Clements compared a community to a superorganism developing toward maturity. Henry Gleason challenged this view in 1926 with his individualistic concept, arguing that each species responds independently to environmental conditions and that communities are loose, chance assemblages rather than tightly integrated units. Most modern ecologists lean toward Gleason's view and recognize that disturbance is so common that many communities never reach a single stable climax. Some ecosystems, such as chaparral shrublands and ponderosa pine forests, are actually maintained by recurring fire, and many of their plants have seeds that germinate only after exposure to heat or smoke.

## Chapter 7: Conservation Biology

### 7.1 The Biodiversity Crisis

Conservation biology is a goal-oriented science that applies ecology, genetics, and other disciplines to protecting biological diversity. Biodiversity is usually considered at three levels: genetic diversity within populations, species diversity within communities, and ecosystem diversity across landscapes. Scientists have described roughly 1.8 to 2 million species, but estimates of the total number on Earth range much higher, with many in the range of 8 to 10 million. Current extinction rates are estimated to be hundreds to thousands of times higher than the background rate seen in the fossil record, leading many researchers to argue that a sixth mass extinction may be under way.

### 7.2 Major Threats

Conservation biologists often group the main threats to biodiversity using the acronym HIPPO: Habitat loss, Invasive species, Pollution, human Population growth, and Overharvesting. Climate change is increasingly treated as a sixth major threat. Habitat loss, from agriculture, logging, and urban development, is considered the single greatest threat to biodiversity worldwide. Habitat fragmentation breaks continuous habitat into smaller, isolated patches, which increases edge effects such as higher predation and drier microclimates along patch borders.

Invasive species are organisms introduced outside their native range that spread and cause harm. The brown tree snake, accidentally introduced to the island of Guam after World War II, eliminated most of the island's native forest bird species. Overharvesting has driven species such as the passenger pigeon to extinction; once possibly the most abundant bird in North America, it was hunted relentlessly, and the last known individual, a captive bird named Martha, died at the Cincinnati Zoo in 1914. The collapse of the northern cod fishery off Newfoundland in the early 1990s, which led Canada to declare a moratorium on cod fishing in 1992, shows that even extremely abundant species can be depleted.

### 7.3 Small Populations and Extinction Risk

Small populations face special dangers. They can enter an extinction vortex, a downward spiral in which low numbers cause inbreeding and loss of genetic diversity through genetic drift, which in turn reduces reproduction and survival, making the population still smaller. Conservation biologists estimate a minimum viable population (MVP), the smallest population size at which a species can persist over a specified time, such as a 95 percent chance of surviving for 100 years. A related concept is the effective population size, Ne, which counts only the individuals that actually contribute genes to the next generation and is often much smaller than the census count.

The greater prairie chicken in Illinois illustrates both the danger and a remedy. Its population fell from millions in the nineteenth century to fewer than 50 birds by 1993, and egg hatching success dropped below 40 percent because of inbreeding. After researchers translocated 271 birds from larger populations in Minnesota, Kansas, and Nebraska to increase genetic variation, hatching success rose to over 90 percent within a few years, a technique now known as genetic rescue.

### 7.4 Protecting Habitats and Landscapes

Because species depend on habitat, much conservation work focuses on landscapes. Biodiversity hotspots are relatively small regions with exceptionally high numbers of endemic species, meaning species found nowhere else, that are also under severe threat. Under the widely used definition, a region must contain at least 1,500 endemic plant species and have lost at least 70 percent of its original vegetation to qualify; the 36 recognized hotspots cover only about 2.5 percent of Earth's land surface yet harbor more than half of the world's endemic plant species. Island biogeography theory, developed by Robert MacArthur and E. O. Wilson in the 1960s, predicts that larger islands and islands closer to a mainland support more species, an idea that has shaped thinking about the design of nature reserves. Movement corridors, strips of habitat connecting otherwise isolated patches, can allow animals to disperse between reserves and reduce inbreeding; highway overpasses built for wildlife in Banff National Park in Canada are a well-known example.

### 7.5 Restoration and Sustainable Development

Restoration ecology uses ecological principles to return degraded ecosystems to a more natural state. Bioremediation uses organisms, usually prokaryotes, fungi, or plants, to detoxify polluted ecosystems; certain bacteria have been used to break down oil spills, and some plants can absorb heavy metals from contaminated soils. Biological augmentation instead uses organisms to add essential materials to a degraded ecosystem, such as planting nitrogen-fixing legumes on depleted mine land.

The broader goal of sustainable development is to meet the needs of people today without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Ecologists contribute to this goal by quantifying ecosystem services, the benefits that people obtain from functioning ecosystems, such as pollination of crops, purification of water by wetlands, flood control, soil formation, and carbon storage in forests. Recognizing the value of these services helps make the case that conserving biodiversity is not only an ethical choice but also a practical investment in human well-being.
