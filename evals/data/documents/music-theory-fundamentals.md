# Music Theory Fundamentals

--- Page 1 ---

Music theory is the study of how music is organized: how sounds are named, combined, and arranged in time. It is not a set of rules that composers must obey, but rather a shared vocabulary for describing patterns that appear again and again in music. This handout covers the foundations used in Western tonal music: pitch, intervals, scales, keys, chords, rhythm and meter, and cadences.

## Pitch

### Frequency and Note Names

Pitch is the perceived highness or lowness of a sound, determined mainly by the frequency of vibration, measured in hertz (Hz), or cycles per second. A faster vibration sounds higher. When one note vibrates at exactly twice the frequency of another, the two sound so similar that they share the same letter name; this relationship is called an octave. The modern tuning standard sets the A above middle C at 440 Hz, so the A one octave higher is 880 Hz and the A one octave lower is 220 Hz. This reference pitch is often called concert pitch or A440.

Western music uses seven letter names for natural notes: A, B, C, D, E, F, and G. After G, the sequence starts again at A. On a piano, these natural notes are played on the white keys. The black keys are named with accidentals. A sharp (♯) raises a note by one half step, a flat (♭) lowers it by one half step, and a natural sign cancels a previous sharp or flat. Two names for the same pitch, such as C♯ and D♭, are called enharmonic equivalents.

### Half Steps and Whole Steps

The smallest interval in standard Western music is the half step, also called a semitone, which is the distance between any key on the piano and the very next key, black or white. Twelve half steps make up one octave. A whole step, or whole tone, equals two half steps. Most adjacent white keys are a whole step apart, but there are two natural half steps with no black key between them: between E and F, and between B and C. In modern instruments, the twelve half steps of the octave are made exactly equal in size, a system called twelve-tone equal temperament, in which each half step multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of two, about 1.0595.

## Intervals

### Size and Quality

An interval is the distance between two pitches. When the two notes sound together, the interval is harmonic; when they sound one after the other, it is melodic. Intervals are named with a number and a quality. The number is found by counting letter names from the lower note to the upper note, including both ends: C up to E counts C, D, E, so it is a third; C up to G is a fifth.

The quality gives the exact size in half steps. Unisons, fourths, fifths, and octaves are called perfect intervals in their most common form. Seconds, thirds, sixths, and sevenths come in major and minor forms, where the minor version is one half step smaller than the major. Any interval made one half step larger than perfect or major becomes augmented, and one made a half step smaller than perfect or minor becomes diminished.

Here are the common intervals measured upward from C, with their sizes in half steps:

| Interval | Example | Half steps |
|---|---|---|
| Minor second | C to D♭ | 1 |
| Major second | C to D | 2 |
| Minor third | C to E♭ | 3 |
| Major third | C to E | 4 |
| Perfect fourth | C to F | 5 |
| Tritone | C to F♯ | 6 |
| Perfect fifth | C to G | 7 |
| Minor sixth | C to A♭ | 8 |
| Major sixth | C to A | 9 |
| Minor seventh | C to B♭ | 10 |
| Major seventh | C to B | 11 |
| Perfect octave | C to C | 12 |

The tritone, spanning six half steps, divides the octave exactly in half; it can be spelled as an augmented fourth or a diminished fifth. Its tense, unstable sound earned it the medieval nickname "the devil in music."

### Consonance and Dissonance

Intervals are often described as consonant, meaning stable and restful, or dissonant, meaning tense and in need of resolution. Perfect unisons, octaves, and fifths, along with major and minor thirds and sixths, are traditionally treated as consonances. Seconds, sevenths, and the tritone are dissonances. Composers use the movement from dissonance to consonance to create a feeling of tension and release.

## Scales

### The Major Scale

A scale is an ordered series of pitches within an octave. The major scale, the foundation of Western tonal music, follows the pattern of whole (W) and half (H) steps W–W–H–W–W–W–H. Starting on C and following this pattern produces C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C, which uses only the white keys of the piano. Starting on any other note requires sharps or flats to keep the pattern intact; for example, the G major scale needs F♯.

Each step of a scale is called a scale degree and has a traditional name. The first degree is the tonic, the home note. The second is the supertonic, the third is the mediant, the fourth is the subdominant, the fifth is the dominant, and the sixth is the submediant. The seventh degree, a half step below the tonic in the major scale, is called the leading tone because it pulls strongly upward toward the tonic.

### Minor Scales

The natural minor scale follows the pattern W–H–W–W–H–W–W. A natural minor scale starting on A uses only white keys: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A. Compared with the major scale built on the same tonic, the natural minor lowers the third, sixth, and seventh degrees, giving it a darker sound.

Because the natural minor scale lacks a leading tone, composers often raise its seventh degree. This produces the harmonic minor scale, which in A minor reads A, B, C, D, E, F, G♯, A. The raised seventh creates an unusual gap of three half steps, an augmented second, between the sixth and seventh degrees, giving the harmonic minor its distinctive exotic sound. To smooth out that gap in melodies, the melodic minor scale raises both the sixth and seventh degrees when ascending, and traditionally returns to the natural minor form when descending.

## Keys and Key Signatures

### Key Signatures

A key is the tonal center of a piece, the scale and tonic around which the music is organized. Rather than writing sharps or flats beside every note, composers place a key signature at the start of each staff. C major and A minor have no sharps or flats. Sharps are added to key signatures in a fixed order, F, C, G, D, A, E, B, and flats in the reverse order, B, E, A, D, G, C, F. A useful trick for sharp keys is that the major key is one half step above the last sharp; with three sharps, the last is G♯, so the key is A major. For flat keys with two or more flats, the second-to-last flat names the major key; with three flats, the second-to-last is E♭, so the key is E♭ major.

### The Circle of Fifths

The circle of fifths arranges the twelve major keys in a circle, each a perfect fifth above the previous one. Moving clockwise from C, the keys are G, D, A, E, B, and F♯, each adding one sharp. Moving counterclockwise from C, the keys are F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, and G♭, each adding one flat. At the bottom of the circle, F♯ major with six sharps and G♭ major with six flats are enharmonic, sounding the same while being spelled differently. Keys next to each other on the circle share all but one note and are called closely related keys, which makes the circle a practical map for changing keys.

### Relative and Parallel Keys

Every major key shares its key signature with a minor key whose tonic lies three half steps below the major tonic. These pairs are called relative keys: C major and A minor are relatives, as are G major and E minor. In contrast, parallel keys share the same tonic but have different key signatures, such as C major and C minor. Moving from one key to another within a piece is called modulation, and a modulation to the relative minor or to the dominant key is among the most common in classical music.

## Chords

### Triads

A chord is three or more pitches sounding together. The most basic chord is the triad, built by stacking two thirds above a starting note called the root. The middle note is the third, and the top note is the fifth. There are four triad qualities. A major triad has a major third on the bottom and a minor third on top, as in C–E–G. A minor triad has a minor third below and a major third above, as in A–C–E. A diminished triad stacks two minor thirds, as in B–D–F. An augmented triad stacks two major thirds, as in C–E–G♯.

### Roman Numeral Analysis

Triads can be built on each degree of a scale, and musicians label them with Roman numerals: uppercase for major, lowercase for minor, and lowercase with a small circle for diminished. In a major key the pattern is I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, and vii°. In C major, that means C major, D minor, E minor, F major, G major, A minor, and B diminished. The three major triads, I, IV, and V, are called the primary chords; together they contain every note of the scale, which is why so many simple songs can be accompanied with just these three chords.

### Seventh Chords

Adding another third on top of a triad creates a seventh chord. The dominant seventh chord, built on the fifth scale degree, combines a major triad with a minor seventh; in C major it is G–B–D–F, labeled V7. Because it contains a tritone between B and F, the dominant seventh is especially unstable and strongly wants to resolve to the tonic chord.

## Rhythm and Meter

### Note Values

Rhythm is the organization of sounds and silences in time. Note values describe relative durations. A whole note is divided into two half notes, a half note into two quarter notes, a quarter note into two eighth notes, and an eighth note into two sixteenth notes. Each note value has a matching rest that indicates silence of the same length. A dot after a note adds half of its value, so a dotted half note equals a half note plus a quarter note, or three quarter notes.

### Beat, Tempo, and Meter

The beat is the steady pulse a listener taps along to. Tempo is the speed of the beat, measured in beats per minute (BPM) and often indicated with Italian terms: largo is very slow, adagio is slow, andante is a walking pace, moderato is moderate, allegro is fast, and presto is very fast. A metronome marking of 60 BPM equals exactly one beat per second.

Meter is the grouping of beats into recurring patterns of strong and weak beats, organized into measures, or bars, separated by bar lines. A time signature at the beginning of the music shows the meter with two numbers. The top number tells how many beats are in each measure, and the bottom number tells which note value receives one beat, with 4 meaning a quarter note and 8 an eighth note. In 4/4 time, also called common time and sometimes written as a C, there are four quarter-note beats per measure, with the strongest accent on beat one. The 3/4 time signature, with three beats per measure, is the meter of the waltz.

### Simple and Compound Meter

In simple meters such as 2/4, 3/4, and 4/4, each beat divides naturally into two equal parts. In compound meters such as 6/8, 9/8, and 12/8, each beat divides into three parts and is represented by a dotted note. Although 6/8 contains six eighth notes per measure, it is usually felt as two beats, each a dotted quarter note, giving it a lilting, rocking feel common in jigs and lullabies.

## Cadences

### What a Cadence Does

A cadence is a harmonic arrival point that marks the end of a musical phrase, much like punctuation marks the end of a clause or sentence in language. Cadences are identified by the final two chords of a phrase, and each type gives a different sense of finality.

### The Four Main Cadence Types

The authentic cadence moves from the dominant chord to the tonic, V to I, and is the strongest and most conclusive ending in tonal music. When both chords are in root position and the melody ends on the tonic note, it is called a perfect authentic cadence; most classical pieces end this way. If any of those conditions is missing, it is an imperfect authentic cadence, which sounds somewhat less final.

The plagal cadence moves from the subdominant chord to the tonic, IV to I. It is softer than the authentic cadence and is often called the "Amen cadence" because it is traditionally sung on the word "Amen" at the end of many hymns.

The half cadence ends on the dominant chord, V, usually approached from I, ii, or IV. Because it stops on an unstable chord, the half cadence sounds like a pause or a question, inviting the music to continue, much like a comma.

The deceptive cadence moves from V to an unexpected chord instead of I, most often to vi. In C major, a G major chord resolving to A minor instead of C major creates a surprising turn that delays the real ending and prolongs the music.
