# Human Nutrition: An Introduction

## What Nutrition Studies

Nutrition is the science of how the body obtains, processes and uses the substances in food. Those substances are called nutrients, and they do three broad jobs: they supply energy, provide building materials for growth and repair, and regulate the chemical reactions of metabolism. Nutrients are usually grouped into six classes: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and water. The first three are the macronutrients, needed in gram quantities and the only ones that provide energy. Vitamins and minerals are micronutrients, needed in milligram or microgram amounts. This handout is educational and general; individual needs vary with age, sex, body size, activity and health, and specific dietary decisions are best made with a qualified professional.

## Macronutrients

### Energy Values

Food energy is measured in kilocalories (kcal), commonly called "calories" on food labels, or in kilojoules (kJ); one kilocalorie equals about 4.184 kilojoules. Each macronutrient yields a characteristic amount of energy when metabolised:

- Carbohydrate: about 4 kcal per gram
- Protein: about 4 kcal per gram
- Fat: about 9 kcal per gram
- Alcohol, which is not a nutrient but does supply energy: about 7 kcal per gram

Because fat provides more than twice the energy of carbohydrate or protein per gram, fatty foods are energy-dense.

### Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are the body's preferred everyday fuel. They are built from sugar units. Monosaccharides, the simplest sugars, include glucose, fructose and galactose. Disaccharides are pairs of these: sucrose (table sugar) is glucose plus fructose, lactose (milk sugar) is glucose plus galactose, and maltose is two glucose units. Polysaccharides are long chains: starch is the storage form of glucose in plants, found in grains, potatoes and legumes, while glycogen is the storage form in animals, kept in the liver and muscles.

The brain is a heavy user of glucose, consuming roughly 120 grams per day in a typical adult. Many guidelines suggest that carbohydrates supply about 45 to 65 percent of daily energy, with an emphasis on whole grains, legumes, vegetables and fruit rather than added sugars. The World Health Organization recommends limiting free sugars to less than 10 percent of total energy intake, and suggests that below 5 percent brings further benefits.

The glycemic index (GI) ranks carbohydrate foods by how quickly they raise blood glucose compared with pure glucose, which is given a value of 100. White bread and cornflakes have a high GI, while lentils and most whole fruits are low. Fibre, fat and protein in a meal tend to slow the rise.

### Proteins

Proteins are chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Human proteins are built from 20 standard amino acids, of which 9 are essential, meaning the body cannot make them and they must come from food: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. Proteins serve as enzymes, hormones such as insulin, structural materials such as collagen and keratin, antibodies, and transport molecules such as haemoglobin.

The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for protein for healthy adults is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, so a 70 kg adult needs about 56 grams. Needs are higher during pregnancy, growth, recovery from illness and in older adults or athletes. Animal proteins such as eggs, milk, meat and fish are "complete", containing all essential amino acids in good proportions. Many plant proteins are low in one essential amino acid, for example grains in lysine and legumes in methionine, but eating a variety of plant foods over the day supplies all of them; this is called protein complementation.

### Fats

Dietary fats, or lipids, include triglycerides, phospholipids and sterols such as cholesterol. A triglyceride consists of a glycerol backbone attached to three fatty acids. Fats store energy, insulate the body, form cell membranes, make some hormones, and are needed to absorb the fat-soluble vitamins.

Fatty acids are classified by their chemical bonds. Saturated fatty acids have no carbon-carbon double bonds and are usually solid at room temperature, as in butter and fatty meat. Monounsaturated fatty acids have one double bond and are abundant in olive oil and avocados. Polyunsaturated fatty acids have two or more and include the omega-3 and omega-6 families. Two polyunsaturated fatty acids are essential: linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3). Oily fish such as salmon and mackerel provide the long-chain omega-3 fats EPA and DHA.

Trans fats, produced mainly by the partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils, raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol and lower HDL ("good") cholesterol. They are strongly linked to heart disease, and many countries have restricted or banned industrially produced trans fats. Guidelines commonly suggest that fat provide 20 to 35 percent of energy, with saturated fat kept below 10 percent.

## Micronutrients

### Vitamins

Vitamins are organic compounds needed in small amounts for normal metabolism. They are divided by solubility. The fat-soluble vitamins are A, D, E and K; they are absorbed with dietary fat and stored in the liver and fatty tissue, so excessive intake, usually from supplements, can accumulate to toxic levels. The water-soluble vitamins are vitamin C and the eight B vitamins; most are not stored in large amounts and excess is generally excreted in urine, so they need regular intake. Vitamin B12 is an exception, as the liver can store several years' supply.

Some important roles and deficiency conditions:

- **Vitamin A** is needed for vision in dim light and for healthy skin and immunity; deficiency causes night blindness and is a leading cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide.
- **Vitamin D** helps the intestine absorb calcium. The skin makes it when exposed to ultraviolet B sunlight. Deficiency causes rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults.
- **Vitamin K** is required to make blood-clotting proteins; newborns are commonly given a vitamin K injection at birth.
- **Vitamin C** (ascorbic acid) is needed to make collagen. Deficiency causes scurvy, with bleeding gums and poor wound healing; in 1747 the naval surgeon James Lind showed in one of the first controlled trials that citrus fruit cured it.
- **Thiamin (B1)** deficiency causes beriberi, which affects the nerves and heart.
- **Niacin (B3)** deficiency causes pellagra, remembered by the "three Ds": dermatitis, diarrhoea and dementia.
- **Folate (B9)** is vital for DNA synthesis. Adequate intake before conception and in early pregnancy reduces the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida, so women who may become pregnant are commonly advised to take 400 micrograms of folic acid daily.
- **Vitamin B12** is found almost only in animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods or supplements. Deficiency causes a megaloblastic anaemia and nerve damage.

### Minerals

Minerals are inorganic elements. Major minerals, needed in amounts above about 100 milligrams per day, include calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, sodium, potassium and chloride. Trace minerals, needed in smaller amounts, include iron, zinc, iodine, selenium, copper and fluoride.

- **Calcium** is the most abundant mineral in the body, with about 99 percent stored in bones and teeth. It is also needed for muscle contraction, nerve signalling and blood clotting. Adults are commonly advised to consume about 1,000 milligrams per day.
- **Iron** is the core of haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in red blood cells. Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world and causes anaemia with fatigue and pallor. Haem iron from meat is absorbed much better than non-haem iron from plants, and vitamin C eaten in the same meal enhances non-haem iron absorption, while tea and coffee reduce it.
- **Iodine** is needed to make thyroid hormones. Deficiency causes goitre, an enlarged thyroid, and during pregnancy can impair a child's brain development. Iodised salt has been the main public health remedy.
- **Sodium** regulates fluid balance and nerve function, but most people eat far more than they need. The WHO recommends less than 2 grams of sodium per day, about 5 grams of salt, because high intake raises blood pressure.
- **Potassium** works with sodium to control fluid balance and blood pressure and is plentiful in fruits, vegetables and beans.

## Energy Balance

Body weight over time reflects energy balance: the relationship between energy taken in from food and drink and energy expended. When intake exceeds expenditure, the surplus is stored, mostly as fat; when expenditure exceeds intake, stores are drawn down.

Total daily energy expenditure has three main components:

1. **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)**, the energy needed to keep the body alive at rest: breathing, circulation, maintaining body temperature and cell activity. BMR typically accounts for about 60 to 70 percent of daily expenditure in a sedentary person, and is higher in people with more lean muscle mass.
2. **The thermic effect of food**, the energy used to digest, absorb and process nutrients, roughly 10 percent of intake. Protein has the highest thermic effect, at around 20 to 30 percent of its own energy content.
3. **Physical activity**, the most variable component, which includes both deliberate exercise and everyday movement such as walking, standing and fidgeting, called non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT).

A pound of body fat is often said to store about 3,500 kcal, but this simple rule overstates long-term weight change, because the body adapts to reduced intake by lowering its energy expenditure.

Body mass index (BMI), weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres, is a common screening tool. For adults, a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 is classified as a healthy weight range, 25 to 29.9 as overweight, and 30 or above as obese. BMI does not distinguish muscle from fat, so it can misclassify very muscular people, and waist circumference adds useful information about abdominal fat.

## Digestion and Absorption

### Along the Digestive Tract

Digestion breaks food into molecules small enough to absorb. It begins in the mouth, where chewing breaks food up and salivary amylase starts splitting starch. In the stomach, hydrochloric acid creates a very acidic environment, with a pH of roughly 1.5 to 3.5, that kills many microbes and activates the enzyme pepsin, which begins protein digestion.

Most digestion and absorption occur in the small intestine, which is about 6 metres long in an adult and divided into the duodenum, jejunum and ileum. The pancreas releases enzymes into the duodenum: pancreatic amylase for starch, lipase for fats, and proteases such as trypsin for proteins. The pancreas also secretes bicarbonate to neutralise stomach acid. Bile, made in the liver and stored in the gallbladder, emulsifies fat into tiny droplets so that lipase can work on a much larger surface area.

### Absorption

The inner wall of the small intestine is folded and covered with finger-like projections called villi, which in turn carry microscopic microvilli. Together these structures give an absorptive surface area often estimated at around 30 square metres, roughly the size of half a badminton court. Sugars and amino acids pass into the blood capillaries of the villi and travel through the hepatic portal vein to the liver. Digested fats are reassembled into particles called chylomicrons, which enter the lymphatic vessels (lacteals) before reaching the bloodstream. Vitamin B12 is absorbed in the final section, the ileum, and requires a protein called intrinsic factor made in the stomach.

The large intestine, or colon, absorbs water and electrolytes and is home to trillions of bacteria, the gut microbiota. These microbes ferment material the small intestine could not digest and produce vitamin K and some B vitamins.

## Dietary Fibre

Dietary fibre consists of plant carbohydrates that human enzymes cannot digest. It is divided into two types:

- **Soluble fibre** dissolves in water to form a gel. It is found in oats, barley, beans, apples and citrus fruits. Beta-glucan from oats and barley can lower LDL cholesterol, and soluble fibre slows the absorption of sugar, smoothing blood glucose after meals.
- **Insoluble fibre** does not dissolve. It is found in wheat bran, whole grains, nuts and vegetable skins. It adds bulk to stool and speeds its passage through the gut, helping prevent constipation.

When gut bacteria ferment fibre, they produce short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, which is the main energy source for the cells lining the colon. Diets high in fibre are associated with lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer. Many health authorities recommend about 25 to 30 grams of fibre per day for adults, while typical intakes in many Western countries are well below that. Increasing fibre gradually, along with fluids, reduces bloating.

## Hydration

Water is the largest component of the body, making up about 50 to 60 percent of body weight in adults, and a higher proportion in infants. It acts as a solvent and transport medium, regulates body temperature through sweating, cushions joints and organs, and takes part in chemical reactions.

Daily water needs vary with climate, activity and body size. The US National Academies set adequate total water intakes of about 3.7 litres per day for men and 2.7 litres per day for women, counting water from all drinks and foods. Roughly 20 percent of total water intake typically comes from food, especially fruits, vegetables and soups. The kidneys adjust urine output to maintain balance, guided by antidiuretic hormone, and pale straw-coloured urine is a simple sign of adequate hydration.

Losing as little as 2 percent of body weight as water can impair physical performance and concentration. Severe dehydration is dangerous, particularly for infants and older adults, whose thirst sensation may be weaker. Drinking very large amounts of plain water in a short time can also be harmful: it can dilute blood sodium, a condition called hyponatraemia, which has occurred in endurance athletes who drank excessively during long events.

## Putting It Together

A healthy dietary pattern typically emphasises vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts and seeds, with moderate amounts of fish, dairy and lean proteins, and limited added sugars, salt, highly processed foods and trans fats. Such a pattern supplies macronutrients in balanced proportions, provides the vitamins, minerals and fibre the body needs, and helps maintain energy balance over time.
