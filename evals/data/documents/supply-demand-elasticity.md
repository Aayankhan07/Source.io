# Supply, Demand, and Elasticity

## 1. Introduction

The model of supply and demand is the central tool economists use to explain how prices are set in competitive markets. It describes the interaction between buyers, who want to pay as little as possible, and sellers, who want to receive as much as possible. The modern graphical version of the model owes much to the British economist Alfred Marshall, whose 1890 textbook Principles of Economics presented supply and demand as two blades of a pair of scissors: asking whether supply or demand determines price, he suggested, is like asking which blade of the scissors does the cutting.

The model works best for markets with many buyers and sellers, a standardized product, and good information, such as markets for wheat, crude oil, or common stocks. It is a simplification, but it explains a remarkable range of real-world events, from rising rents in growing cities to the effects of a frost on the price of orange juice.

## 2. Demand

### 2.1 The law of demand

Demand is the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity that buyers are willing and able to purchase during a given period, holding all other factors constant. The law of demand states that, other things equal, as the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls. Plotted with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, the demand curve therefore slopes downward.

Three reasons explain the downward slope:

- **Substitution effect:** when a good becomes more expensive relative to alternatives, consumers switch to substitutes.
- **Income effect:** a higher price reduces the purchasing power of a consumer's income, so they can afford less.
- **Diminishing marginal utility:** each additional unit of a good tends to provide less extra satisfaction, so buyers will purchase more only at lower prices.

### 2.2 Determinants of demand

Besides the good's own price, several factors influence how much people want to buy. A change in any of them shifts the entire demand curve:

1. **Income.** For a normal good, higher income increases demand. For an inferior good, such as generic store brands or long-distance bus travel, higher income decreases demand as people trade up.
2. **Prices of related goods.** A rise in the price of a substitute (for example, tea relative to coffee) increases demand for the other good. A rise in the price of a complement (for example, printers and ink cartridges) decreases demand for the other good.
3. **Tastes and preferences**, influenced by advertising, fashion, or health information.
4. **Expectations** about future prices or income. If buyers expect prices to rise next month, demand increases today.
5. **Number of buyers** in the market, affected by population growth or new export markets.

## 3. Supply

### 3.1 The law of supply

Supply is the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity that producers are willing and able to sell. The law of supply states that, other things equal, a higher price leads to a larger quantity supplied, so the supply curve slopes upward. Higher prices make production more profitable, encourage existing firms to expand output, and draw in new producers. Rising marginal costs also mean firms need higher prices to justify producing additional units.

### 3.2 Determinants of supply

The supply curve shifts when any of the following change:

1. **Input prices.** Higher wages, raw material costs, or energy prices reduce supply (shift the curve left).
2. **Technology.** Improvements that lower production costs increase supply (shift it right).
3. **Number of sellers.**
4. **Expectations** about future prices. A farmer expecting higher prices next season may store grain rather than sell now.
5. **Government policy**, including taxes, which reduce supply, and subsidies, which increase it.
6. **Natural conditions** such as weather, which matter greatly in agriculture.

## 4. Market Equilibrium

Equilibrium occurs at the price where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. On a graph, it is the point where the two curves cross. The price there is called the equilibrium or market-clearing price.

If the price is above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, creating a **surplus**. Sellers with unsold inventory cut prices, pushing the market back toward equilibrium. If the price is below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, creating a **shortage**. Buyers compete for scarce goods and sellers raise prices. These self-correcting pressures are what Adam Smith's "invisible hand" image is often used to describe.

### 4.1 A numerical example

Suppose the demand for concert T-shirts is given by Qd = 200 − 4P and the supply by Qs = 20 + 2P, where P is the price in dollars. Setting Qd = Qs gives 200 − 4P = 20 + 2P, so 180 = 6P and P = 30 dollars. Substituting back, Q = 200 − 120 = 80 shirts. At a price of 40 dollars, quantity demanded would be 40 and quantity supplied 100, a surplus of 60 shirts.

## 5. Shifts Versus Movements Along a Curve

One of the most important distinctions in introductory economics is between a change in quantity demanded and a change in demand.

- A **movement along the demand curve** (a change in quantity demanded) is caused only by a change in the good's own price.
- A **shift of the demand curve** (a change in demand) is caused by a change in any other determinant, such as income or the price of a related good.

The same distinction applies to supply. A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the supply curve, while a change in input costs or technology shifts the whole curve.

### 5.1 Predicting the effects of shifts

| Event | Effect on equilibrium price | Effect on equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases | Rises | Falls |

When both curves shift at the same time, the direction of one variable is certain but the other is ambiguous. For example, if demand and supply both increase, equilibrium quantity definitely rises, but price may rise, fall, or stay the same depending on which shift is larger.

A classic example: a severe frost in Florida destroys part of the orange crop. Supply of oranges shifts left, so the price of oranges rises and quantity falls. Because orange juice producers face higher input costs, the supply of orange juice also shifts left. Meanwhile, the demand for apple juice, a substitute, shifts right.

## 6. Price Elasticity of Demand

Knowing the direction of a change is often not enough; we also need to know its size. Elasticity measures responsiveness. The price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, the result is negative, but economists usually report its absolute value.

### 6.1 The midpoint method

Percentage changes depend on the starting point, so economists often use the midpoint (arc) formula, which divides each change by the average of the starting and ending values. For example, if the price of a museum ticket rises from 8 to 12 dollars and visits fall from 1,100 to 900 per day, the percentage change in quantity is 200 / 1,000 = 20%, and the percentage change in price is 4 / 10 = 40%. The elasticity is 20% / 40% = 0.5.

### 6.2 Categories of elasticity

- **Elastic demand (elasticity greater than 1):** quantity responds more than proportionally to price. Typical of goods with many close substitutes, such as a particular brand of breakfast cereal.
- **Inelastic demand (elasticity less than 1):** quantity responds less than proportionally. Typical of necessities like insulin, tap water, or gasoline in the short run.
- **Unit elastic (elasticity exactly 1):** percentage changes are equal.
- **Perfectly inelastic (elasticity of 0):** a vertical demand curve; quantity does not change at all.
- **Perfectly elastic (infinite elasticity):** a horizontal demand curve, as faced by a single wheat farmer in a large competitive market.

### 6.3 Determinants of price elasticity

Demand tends to be more elastic when close substitutes are available, when the good is a luxury rather than a necessity, when the market is narrowly defined (demand for "Brand X jeans" is more elastic than demand for "clothing"), when the good takes up a large share of a household budget, and when consumers have more time to adjust. Gasoline demand, for instance, is quite inelastic within a few weeks but becomes more elastic over several years as people buy fuel-efficient cars or move closer to work.

### 6.4 Elasticity and total revenue

Total revenue equals price times quantity. Elasticity tells a seller how revenue will respond to a price change. When demand is inelastic, raising the price increases total revenue, because the loss in quantity is proportionally smaller than the price increase. When demand is elastic, raising the price reduces total revenue. When demand is unit elastic, total revenue stays the same. This is why farmers as a group can be worse off after a bumper harvest: demand for staple crops is inelastic, so the large drop in price more than offsets the increase in quantity sold.

### 6.5 Other elasticities

The **income elasticity of demand** is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income; it is positive for normal goods and negative for inferior goods. The **cross-price elasticity** measures how the quantity demanded of one good responds to the price of another; it is positive for substitutes and negative for complements. The **price elasticity of supply** measures how strongly quantity supplied responds to price, and it is usually larger in the long run because firms can build new factories.

## 7. Price Controls

Governments sometimes override market prices with legal limits.

### 7.1 Price ceilings

A price ceiling is a legal maximum price. It is binding only if it is set below the equilibrium price. A binding ceiling creates a shortage because quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the controlled price. Common side effects include long queues, waiting lists, black markets, and a decline in quality as sellers cut costs. Rent control is the standard example: by holding rents below market levels, it can reduce the supply and upkeep of rental housing over time. The gasoline price controls in the United States in the 1970s produced long lines at filling stations.

### 7.2 Price floors

A price floor is a legal minimum price. It is binding only if it is set above the equilibrium price, and a binding floor creates a surplus. Agricultural price supports have historically led governments to buy and store surplus crops such as butter and milk powder. The minimum wage is a price floor in the labor market; if set above the equilibrium wage, the standard model predicts that the quantity of labor supplied exceeds the quantity demanded, causing some unemployment among low-skilled workers, although empirical studies disagree about how large this effect is in practice.

### 7.3 Deadweight loss

Both binding ceilings and floors reduce the quantity traded below the efficient equilibrium level. The value of the trades that no longer happen is called deadweight loss, a net loss of total surplus to society. The more elastic supply and demand are, the larger the deadweight loss from a given price control, because quantities respond more strongly to the distorted price.

## 8. Summary

Demand slopes down and supply slopes up, and their intersection determines the equilibrium price and quantity. Changes in a good's own price cause movements along a curve, while changes in other factors shift the curve. Price elasticity of demand measures how strongly buyers respond to price changes and determines whether a price increase raises or lowers total revenue. Price ceilings below equilibrium cause shortages, price floors above equilibrium cause surpluses, and both create deadweight loss.
