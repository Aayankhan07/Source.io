# Formation of Contracts in Common-Law Systems

## What Is a Contract?

A contract is a legally enforceable agreement between two or more parties that creates obligations each party can be required to perform. In common-law systems, such as those of England and Wales, the United States, Canada, Australia, and India, contract law developed mainly through judicial decisions rather than through a single comprehensive code, although statutes now govern many specific areas. In the United States, for example, contracts for the sale of goods are governed by Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), while most other contracts remain subject to common-law rules, many of which are summarized in the Restatement (Second) of Contracts.

Not every promise is a contract. A promise to meet a friend for lunch, for example, is normally not enforceable in court. For an agreement to qualify as a binding contract, the traditional common-law analysis requires several elements:

1. **Offer**: a clear proposal of terms by one party.
2. **Acceptance**: an unqualified agreement to those terms by the other party.
3. **Consideration**: something of value exchanged by each side.
4. **Intention to create legal relations**: the parties must intend the agreement to have legal consequences.
5. **Capacity**: each party must be legally competent to contract.
6. **Certainty of terms**: the essential terms must be sufficiently definite.

Even if all elements are present, a contract can be undermined by defenses such as misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, mistake, or illegality. This handout covers each element and the main defenses in turn.

## Offer

### Definition

An offer is an expression of willingness to contract on specified terms, made with the intention that it will become binding as soon as the person to whom it is addressed (the offeree) accepts it. The person making the offer is the offeror. Courts judge intention objectively: the question is not what the offeror secretly meant, but how a reasonable person in the offeree's position would understand the words and conduct.

### Offers versus invitations to treat

An offer must be distinguished from an invitation to treat (in American terminology, an invitation to bargain or a preliminary negotiation). An invitation to treat is merely an indication that a party is willing to receive offers. Common examples include:

- **Goods displayed in a shop**: the display, even with a price tag, is generally an invitation to treat. The customer makes the offer at the checkout, and the shop accepts it.
- **Advertisements**: most newspaper or online advertisements for goods are invitations to treat, because the seller could not supply unlimited quantities to everyone who responds.
- **Auctions**: the auctioneer's request for bids is an invitation to treat; each bid is an offer, and acceptance occurs when the hammer falls. In an auction "without reserve," however, many jurisdictions treat the auctioneer as bound to sell to the highest genuine bidder.
- **Requests for tenders**: an invitation to submit tenders is usually an invitation to treat, and each tender is an offer.

An important exception concerns unilateral offers, in which a person promises something in return for the performance of an act, such as a reward for finding a lost dog or a promise to pay anyone who uses a product as directed and still falls ill. Such an advertisement can be a genuine offer to the world at large, accepted by anyone who performs the requested act.

### Termination of an offer

An offer does not remain open forever. It can end in several ways:

- **Revocation**: the offeror may withdraw the offer at any time before acceptance, but revocation is effective only when it is communicated to the offeree. Communication may come from a reliable third party.
- **Rejection**: if the offeree rejects the offer, it ends and cannot later be accepted.
- **Counter-offer**: a response that changes the terms is treated as a rejection of the original offer plus a new offer.
- **Lapse of time**: an offer lapses at the end of any stated time limit or, if none is stated, after a reasonable time.
- **Death or incapacity**: the death of either party usually terminates an offer, at least once the other party knows of it.

A promise to keep an offer open is not binding unless the offeree gives consideration for it, creating an option contract. Under UCC section 2-205, however, a merchant's signed written promise to keep an offer for goods open, called a firm offer, is binding without consideration for the stated period, up to a maximum of three months.

## Acceptance

### The mirror image rule

Acceptance is a final and unqualified assent to the terms of an offer. Traditionally, the acceptance must match the offer exactly, a principle known as the mirror image rule. If the offeree adds new terms or alters existing ones, the response is a counter-offer rather than an acceptance. A mere request for information, such as asking whether delivery could be made earlier, does not reject the offer.

In commercial practice, businesses often exchange standard forms whose printed terms conflict, a situation called the battle of the forms. Under the traditional common-law approach, the last document sent before performance begins often prevails, the so-called "last shot" rule. UCC section 2-207 modifies the mirror image rule for sales of goods, so that a definite acceptance can create a contract even if it contains additional terms.

### Communication of acceptance

In general, acceptance must be communicated to the offeror, and silence does not amount to acceptance. An offeror cannot impose a contract by stating that "if I hear nothing from you, I will assume you accept." The offeror can, however, prescribe a particular method of acceptance, and an offeree using another method that is equally fast and effective will usually still be bound.

For unilateral contracts, the offeror typically waives the need for communication; performing the requested act is itself the acceptance.

### The postal rule

A special rule applies to acceptances sent by post. Under the postal rule, also called the mailbox rule, an acceptance sent by post is effective as soon as the letter is properly posted, not when it arrives, provided that use of the post was reasonable. This means a contract can be formed even if the letter is delayed or lost. The rule does not apply to revocations, which take effect only when received, and it can be excluded by the terms of the offer. Courts have generally declined to extend the postal rule to instantaneous forms of communication such as telephone, telex, and in most cases email, where acceptance is usually effective when it is received.

## Consideration

### Definition

Consideration is the price for which the promise of the other party is bought. Each party must give or promise something of legal value, which can be a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee. Consideration may take the form of money, goods, services, a promise to act, or a promise to refrain from acting, such as giving up a legal right. A purely gratuitous promise, like a promise to give someone a birthday present, lacks consideration and is unenforceable unless it is made in a deed, a formal written document signed, witnessed, and delivered.

### Rules of consideration

Several well-established rules govern consideration:

- **Consideration must be sufficient but need not be adequate.** Courts do not ask whether the bargain is fair; a nominal amount, such as one dollar or a peppercorn, can support a promise, as long as it has some value in the eyes of the law.
- **Past consideration is not good consideration.** If an act was already completed before the promise was made, it cannot usually count as consideration for that promise. An exception exists where the act was performed at the promisor's request and both sides understood that payment would follow.
- **Consideration must move from the promisee.** A person can enforce a promise only if they themselves provided consideration, although statutes in many jurisdictions now allow certain third-party beneficiaries to enforce contract terms.
- **Performing an existing duty is usually not consideration.** Doing something one is already legally or contractually obliged to do generally cannot support a new promise. Some courts have softened this rule where the promisor obtains a practical benefit, such as avoiding the cost of finding a replacement contractor.

### Promissory estoppel

Where there is no consideration, a party may still obtain relief through promissory estoppel. In the United States, section 90 of the Restatement (Second) of Contracts allows a promise to be enforced when the promisor should reasonably expect it to induce action or forbearance, the promisee does rely on it, and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. In English law, the doctrine is narrower: it generally acts as "a shield, not a sword," preventing a party from going back on a promise not to enforce strict legal rights rather than creating a new cause of action.

## Intention to Create Legal Relations

The law presumes different things depending on context. In social and domestic agreements, such as arrangements between spouses, parents and children, or friends, there is a rebuttable presumption that the parties did not intend legal consequences. In commercial agreements, the presumption runs the other way: parties are presumed to intend legal relations, and the burden of proving otherwise is heavy. Business parties can rebut the presumption with clear language, such as an "honour clause" stating that an agreement is binding in honour only. Letters of comfort and statements that a document is "subject to contract" also often indicate that no binding obligation is yet intended.

## Capacity

Capacity refers to the legal ability to enter a binding contract. Certain categories of people have limited capacity:

- **Minors**: in most common-law jurisdictions, the age of majority is 18. Contracts with minors are generally voidable at the minor's option, meaning the minor may enforce or escape them. However, minors are bound to pay a reasonable price for necessaries, which are goods and services suitable to their condition in life and actual needs, such as food, clothing, and basic accommodation. Beneficial contracts of employment or apprenticeship can also bind a minor.
- **Mentally incapacitated persons**: a contract may be voidable if a person, because of mental illness or disability, could not understand the nature of the transaction and the other party knew or ought to have known this.
- **Intoxicated persons**: similar rules apply where a person was so drunk or drugged that they could not understand what they were doing, and the other party was aware of it.
- **Corporations**: historically limited by the ultra vires doctrine to acts within their stated objects, though modern company legislation has largely removed this restriction for dealings with outsiders.

## Certainty and Formalities

The terms of a contract must be sufficiently certain for a court to enforce them. An agreement to agree on a price in the future, without any mechanism for setting it, is typically too vague. Courts can fill gaps by implying reasonable terms, especially where the parties have already performed, and in sales of goods the UCC supplies default terms such as a reasonable price.

Most contracts need no particular form and may be oral. Some types, however, must be in writing or evidenced in writing. Many jurisdictions trace this requirement back to the English Statute of Frauds of 1677. In the United States, the Statute of Frauds typically covers contracts for the sale of land, contracts that cannot be performed within one year, promises to answer for the debt of another (guarantees), contracts made in consideration of marriage, and, under UCC section 2-201, sales of goods priced at $500 or more.

## Vitiating Factors and Defenses

A contract that is formally valid can still be attacked if consent was defective. Depending on the defense, the contract may be void (of no legal effect from the start), voidable (valid until the innocent party chooses to set it aside, a remedy called rescission), or unenforceable.

### Misrepresentation

A misrepresentation is a false statement of fact or law, made by one party to the other before the contract, that induces the other party to enter it. Statements of opinion, mere sales talk (puffery) such as "the best coffee in town," and statements of future intention are generally not representations of fact. Misrepresentation is classified as fraudulent (made knowingly or recklessly), negligent, or innocent. Rescission is the main remedy, and damages may also be available, especially for fraud. Silence is usually not misrepresentation, but a half-truth or a failure to correct a statement that has become false may be.

### Mistake

Mistake can make a contract void in narrow circumstances. A common mistake, shared by both parties about a fundamental fact, such as the subject matter having already been destroyed before the contract was made, can void the contract. A unilateral mistake, made by only one party, rarely affects validity unless the other party knew of it or it concerned the identity of the other party where identity was crucial.

### Duress and undue influence

Duress is illegitimate pressure that leaves a party no practical choice but to agree. It originally covered threats of physical violence but now also includes economic duress, such as an unlawful threat to break a contract unless the other party pays more. Undue influence is an equitable doctrine that applies where one party exploits a relationship of trust and confidence, such as between a solicitor and client or a religious adviser and follower. Both make the contract voidable.

### Illegality and public policy

Contracts to commit crimes or torts, or that are contrary to public policy, are generally unenforceable. Examples include agreements to fix prices in violation of competition law and unreasonable restraint of trade clauses that stop an employee from working in a field for an excessive time or area. Courts may enforce a restraint of trade only if it protects a legitimate interest and is reasonable in duration and geographic scope.

## Summary

Contract formation in common-law systems rests on a few core ideas: a definite offer, a matching acceptance properly communicated, consideration on both sides, an intention to be legally bound, and parties with legal capacity. Courts assess these objectively. Even a properly formed contract can be set aside where consent was obtained through misrepresentation, mistake, duress, or undue influence, or where enforcing it would be illegal or contrary to public policy.
