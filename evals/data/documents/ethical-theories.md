## What Normative Ethics Asks

Normative ethics is the part of moral philosophy that tries to answer the question "What makes actions right or wrong?" It sits between metaethics, which asks what moral claims mean and whether they can be true, and applied ethics, which tackles specific issues such as euthanasia, animal welfare or business conduct. A normative theory aims to provide a general account that explains our firmest moral judgments and helps us reason about hard cases.

Four families of theory dominate the field today: consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics and contractualism. Each focuses on a different feature of moral life. Consequentialism looks at outcomes, deontology at duties and rights, virtue ethics at character, and contractualism at principles that people could agree to. Philosophers often test these theories with thought experiments: short imagined scenarios designed to isolate a single moral variable and see how each theory handles it.

## Consequentialism

### The Core Idea

Consequentialism holds that the rightness of an action depends only on its consequences. The right act, in the simplest version, is the one that produces the best overall state of affairs among the options available. Nothing is forbidden in itself; lying, breaking promises or even harming someone could be right if doing so led to better results than any alternative.

### Classical Utilitarianism

The most influential form of consequentialism is utilitarianism. Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) argued that pleasure and pain are the only things good or bad in themselves, and that the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Bentham even proposed a "felicific calculus" for measuring pleasures along dimensions such as intensity, duration, certainty and how soon they arrive.

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) defended utilitarianism in his 1863 book Utilitarianism, but he distinguished between higher pleasures, such as intellectual and moral satisfactions, and lower, bodily pleasures. Mill famously claimed that it is better to be a dissatisfied human than a satisfied pig, meaning that quality as well as quantity of pleasure matters.

Classical utilitarianism has three key features. It is welfarist, because only well-being counts in assessing outcomes. It is aggregative, because it adds up welfare across everyone affected. And it is impartial, because each person's well-being counts equally, with no extra weight for oneself, one's family or one's country. Peter Singer has used this impartiality to argue that wealthy people have strong obligations to donate to effective charities.

### Act and Rule Utilitarianism

Act utilitarianism evaluates each individual action directly by its consequences. Rule utilitarianism instead asks which general rules would produce the best consequences if everyone accepted them, and then judges an act right if it follows those rules. A rule utilitarian might say that a rule against punishing the innocent produces far better results overall than a rule permitting it, even if breaking the rule would occasionally produce a slightly better outcome. Critics reply that rule utilitarianism either collapses into act utilitarianism, once the rules are given enough exceptions, or becomes a kind of "rule worship" that ignores the theory's own goal.

### Objections to Consequentialism

Utilitarianism faces several well-known objections. It seems too demanding, because almost any spare money or time could produce more good if given away. It can appear to ignore the separateness of persons, treating one person's suffering as acceptable if it buys a larger total gain for others. And it seems to permit terrible acts when the numbers favour them.

A classic illustration is the transplant case. Imagine a surgeon with five patients, each of whom will die without a different organ, and a healthy visitor whose organs would match all five. Killing the visitor and distributing the organs would save five lives at the cost of one. A crude calculation recommends the killing, yet nearly everyone judges it monstrous. Defenders respond that a world where hospitals did this would generate fear and distrust, so the real consequences would be bad, but many critics feel this misses the point: the act seems wrong because of what it does to the visitor, not because of its side effects.

Robert Nozick's "experience machine", proposed in 1974, targets the hedonistic theory of value behind classical utilitarianism. Suppose a machine could give you any pleasant experiences you wanted for the rest of your life while you floated in a tank, believing it all to be real. Most people say they would not plug in, which suggests that we value things beyond pleasant experiences, such as actually doing things and being in contact with reality.

## Deontology

### Duties and Constraints

Deontology, from the Greek word for duty, holds that some actions are required or forbidden regardless of their consequences. Deontologists typically recognise constraints, such as prohibitions on killing the innocent, lying or breaking promises, that may not be violated even to bring about a better outcome. They also usually recognise options or prerogatives: room to pursue one's own projects instead of always maximising the good.

### Kant's Ethics

The most influential deontologist is Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), particularly in his Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785). Kant held that the only thing good without qualification is a good will: a will that acts from duty, out of respect for the moral law, rather than from inclination or self-interest. A shopkeeper who gives correct change only because honesty is good for business acts in accordance with duty but not from duty, and so his action lacks genuine moral worth.

Kant distinguished hypothetical imperatives, which tell you what to do if you want some goal ("If you want to be healthy, exercise"), from the categorical imperative, which binds all rational beings unconditionally. He offered several formulations of it:

- **The Formula of Universal Law**: act only on a maxim (a personal rule of action) that you could at the same time will to become a universal law. A false promise to repay a loan fails the test, because if everyone made false promises when convenient, the practice of promising would collapse and the maxim would undermine itself.
- **The Formula of Humanity**: always treat humanity, whether in yourself or in others, as an end in itself and never merely as a means. Using someone as a tool without their possible consent, as in deception or coercion, violates their dignity as a rational agent.
- **The Formula of the Kingdom of Ends**: act as if you were a legislating member of an ideal community in which every rational being is both a lawmaker and subject to the law.

### Objections and Refinements

Kant's view is often criticised for rigidity. In a well-known exchange with Benjamin Constant, Kant maintained that it would be wrong to lie even to a murderer at the door who asks where your friend is hiding. Most readers find this conclusion hard to accept. Another problem is conflicting duties: if absolute duties clash, a strictly absolutist theory gives no guidance.

The Scottish philosopher W. D. Ross (1877–1971) responded with a theory of prima facie duties. Ross listed several duties, including fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement and non-maleficence. Each is binding unless outweighed by a stronger duty in the circumstances, and judging which duty prevails in a particular case requires practical wisdom rather than a formula.

### The Trolley Problem

The trolley problem was introduced by Philippa Foot in 1967 and developed by Judith Jarvis Thomson. In the basic version, a runaway tram is heading toward five workers on the track. You can pull a lever to divert it onto a side track where it will kill one worker. Most people say diverting is permissible. In Thomson's footbridge variant, the only way to stop the tram is to push a large stranger off a bridge into its path, killing him but saving the five. The numbers are identical, yet most people judge pushing wrong.

Consequentialists struggle to explain the difference, since five lives are saved for one in both cases. Deontologists appeal to the doctrine of double effect, associated with Thomas Aquinas, which distinguishes harm intended as a means from harm merely foreseen as a side effect. In the footbridge case the stranger's death is the means of saving the five; in the lever case the one worker's death is an unwanted side effect.

## Virtue Ethics

### Character First

Virtue ethics shifts the central question from "What should I do?" to "What kind of person should I be?" Its roots lie in ancient Greek philosophy, especially Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics. For Aristotle, the highest human good is eudaimonia, often translated as flourishing or living well, rather than mere pleasure. Human beings flourish by exercising their characteristic capacity, reason, well over a complete life.

A virtue is a stable trait of character, such as courage, honesty, generosity or justice, that disposes a person to perceive, feel and act well. Virtues are acquired through habituation: just as one becomes a builder by building, one becomes brave by repeatedly doing brave acts until they become second nature.

### The Doctrine of the Mean

Aristotle described many virtues as a mean between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency. Courage lies between rashness (too much confidence) and cowardice (too much fear). Generosity lies between wastefulness and stinginess. The mean is not a mathematical midpoint but what is appropriate "relative to us" and the situation: the courageous response to a burning building differs for a trained firefighter and a small child.

Finding the mean requires phronesis, or practical wisdom: the ability to see what a situation calls for and to deliberate well about how to act. Practical wisdom is what allows the virtues to work together sensibly in real life.

### Contemporary Virtue Ethics

Virtue ethics revived in the twentieth century. Elizabeth Anscombe's 1958 essay "Modern Moral Philosophy" argued that talk of moral obligation had lost its foundations and urged a return to concepts like character and virtue. Later authors such as Alasdair MacIntyre, Philippa Foot and Rosalind Hursthouse developed the approach further. Hursthouse proposed that an action is right if it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances.

### Objections

Critics argue that virtue ethics gives too little action guidance: telling someone facing a hard dilemma to "do what a virtuous person would do" may not help if they do not know what that is. Defenders reply that the virtues themselves supply guidance in the form of "v-rules", such as "be honest" and "do not be cruel". A second challenge, called situationism, draws on social psychology experiments suggesting that behaviour depends more on circumstances than on stable traits. In one well-known study, seminary students in a hurry were much less likely to stop and help a person slumped in a doorway than those who had time to spare.

## Contractualism

### Morality as Agreement

Contractualism grounds morality in the idea of agreement among people who see one another as equals. Its ancestor is social contract theory, developed by Thomas Hobbes, John Locke and Jean-Jacques Rousseau to justify political authority. Hobbes argued that without government, life in the state of nature would be "nasty, brutish and short", so rational people would agree to give up some freedom in exchange for security.

### Rawls and the Veil of Ignorance

John Rawls, in A Theory of Justice (1971), applied contract reasoning to the principles of a just society. He asked us to imagine people choosing principles from behind a veil of ignorance, in an "original position" where nobody knows their own sex, race, talents, wealth or conception of the good life. Because no one could tailor the rules to benefit themselves, the principles chosen would be fair. Rawls argued that such people would choose equal basic liberties for all and the difference principle, which allows social and economic inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged members of society.

### Scanlon's Contractualism

T. M. Scanlon's 1998 book What We Owe to Each Other offers a contractualist account of individual morality. On his view, an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject. Crucially, objections are assessed person by person rather than added up. A principle that imposes a severe burden on one person cannot be justified simply because it gives many others small benefits. This lets contractualism resist the aggregation that leads utilitarianism to troubling conclusions, as in the transplant case.

Contractualism has its own difficulties. It is unclear how it protects beings who cannot take part in agreements, such as animals, infants or future generations. And deciding what counts as "reasonable" rejection may already require the moral judgments the theory was meant to explain.

## Comparing the Theories

A useful way to compare the four families is to ask what each treats as fundamental:

| Theory | Central focus | Key figures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consequentialism | Best outcomes | Bentham, Mill, Singer |
| Deontology | Duties, rights and constraints | Kant, Ross |
| Virtue ethics | Character and flourishing | Aristotle, Anscombe, Hursthouse |
| Contractualism | Justifiability to each person | Hobbes, Rawls, Scanlon |

In practice, many philosophers are pluralists who believe each theory captures something important. Consequences clearly matter, there seem to be some things we must not do to people, good character shapes how we see and respond to situations, and moral rules should be ones we could justify to everyone affected. Derek Parfit's later work even argued that the best versions of Kantian, contractualist and rule consequentialist theories converge, describing them as "climbing the same mountain on different sides". Studying the theories side by side, and testing them against thought experiments like the trolley problem, the transplant case and the experience machine, sharpens our understanding of why we hold the moral views we do.
