## Purpose of This Handout

This handout introduces the basic physiology of the heart and blood vessels for students in introductory health science courses. It explains how the heart is built, how it fills and empties during each beat, how blood pressure is generated and regulated, and what happens when pressure stays too high for too long. It is educational material only and is not medical advice; anyone with concerns about their own heart or blood pressure should consult a qualified clinician.

## Overview of the Cardiovascular System

The cardiovascular system consists of the heart, the blood vessels, and the blood. Its central job is transport: it carries oxygen from the lungs and nutrients from the gut to every tissue, and it removes carbon dioxide and metabolic wastes. It also distributes hormones, helps regulate body temperature, and carries immune cells.

An average adult has about 5 liters of blood. At rest, the heart pumps roughly that entire volume around the body every minute. Over a lifetime of about 80 years, the heart beats more than 2.5 billion times.

The system is arranged as two circuits in series:

- The **pulmonary circulation** carries blood from the right side of the heart to the lungs, where it releases carbon dioxide and picks up oxygen, then returns it to the left side of the heart.
- The **systemic circulation** carries oxygen-rich blood from the left side of the heart to the rest of the body and returns oxygen-poor blood to the right side.

Because the two circuits are in series, the right and left sides of the heart must pump the same volume of blood per minute over time.

## Anatomy of the Heart

The heart is a muscular organ about the size of a closed fist, weighing roughly 250 to 350 grams in adults. It sits in the mediastinum, the central compartment of the chest, tilted so that its pointed apex lies toward the left. It is enclosed in a double-layered sac called the pericardium, which contains a small amount of lubricating fluid.

### Layers of the Heart Wall

The heart wall has three layers. The outer epicardium is a thin protective layer. The middle myocardium is the thick layer of cardiac muscle that does the pumping. The inner endocardium lines the chambers and valves with smooth tissue that reduces friction with the blood.

### The Four Chambers

The heart has four chambers. The two upper chambers, the right and left atria, receive blood returning to the heart. The two lower chambers, the right and left ventricles, pump blood out of the heart. A muscular wall called the septum separates the right and left sides.

The path of blood through the heart is as follows. Oxygen-poor blood from the body enters the right atrium through the superior and inferior venae cavae. It passes into the right ventricle, which pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs. Oxygen-rich blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium, flows into the left ventricle, and is ejected into the aorta, the body's largest artery.

The wall of the left ventricle is about three times thicker than that of the right ventricle, because it must generate enough pressure to push blood through the entire systemic circulation, whereas the right ventricle only needs to push blood through the nearby low-resistance lungs.

### The Valves

Four valves keep blood flowing in one direction:

| Valve | Location | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Tricuspid | Between right atrium and right ventricle | Atrioventricular |
| Mitral (bicuspid) | Between left atrium and left ventricle | Atrioventricular |
| Pulmonary | Between right ventricle and pulmonary artery | Semilunar |
| Aortic | Between left ventricle and aorta | Semilunar |

The atrioventricular valves are anchored by thin cords called chordae tendineae, attached to papillary muscles in the ventricle wall; these prevent the valve flaps from turning inside out when the ventricle contracts. The familiar "lub-dub" sound of the heartbeat comes from valves closing: the first sound (S1, "lub") is the closing of the atrioventricular valves, and the second sound (S2, "dub") is the closing of the semilunar valves.

### Coronary Circulation

Although the heart is full of blood, the myocardium cannot absorb enough oxygen from the blood in its chambers. It has its own blood supply, the coronary arteries, which branch from the base of the aorta. The two main vessels are the left and right coronary arteries. A blockage in a coronary artery deprives part of the heart muscle of oxygen and can cause a myocardial infarction, commonly called a heart attack.

## The Electrical Conduction System

Cardiac muscle contracts in response to electrical signals generated within the heart itself, a property called autorhythmicity. The heart therefore keeps beating even when nerves to it are cut, as happens in a heart transplant.

The sequence of the conduction system is:

1. The **sinoatrial (SA) node**, in the wall of the right atrium, spontaneously generates impulses and is the heart's natural pacemaker. It typically fires 60 to 100 times per minute at rest.
2. The impulse spreads across both atria, causing them to contract.

--- Page 3 ---

3. The **atrioventricular (AV) node** delays the impulse by about 0.1 second, giving the atria time to finish emptying into the ventricles.
4. The impulse travels down the **bundle of His** and its left and right bundle branches in the septum.
5. **Purkinje fibers** distribute the signal throughout the ventricular walls, producing a coordinated contraction that squeezes blood upward toward the outflow valves.

An electrocardiogram (ECG) records this electrical activity from the skin. The P wave represents atrial depolarization, the QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization, and the T wave represents ventricular repolarization. Atrial repolarization is hidden within the larger QRS complex.

## The Cardiac Cycle

The cardiac cycle is the sequence of events in one heartbeat. Contraction is called systole and relaxation is called diastole. At a heart rate of 75 beats per minute, one cycle lasts about 0.8 seconds, of which ventricular systole occupies about 0.3 seconds and diastole about 0.5 seconds. Because diastole shortens more than systole as heart rate rises, very fast rates can leave too little time for the ventricles to fill.

The main phases are:

- **Ventricular filling (diastole).** The atrioventricular valves are open and blood flows passively from the atria into the ventricles. About 70 to 80 percent of filling happens passively; the atrial contraction at the end of diastole, sometimes called the atrial kick, adds the remainder.
- **Isovolumetric contraction.** The ventricles begin to contract, pressure rises, and the atrioventricular valves close. For a brief moment all four valves are closed, so volume does not change.
- **Ventricular ejection.** When ventricular pressure exceeds the pressure in the aorta and pulmonary artery, the semilunar valves open and blood is ejected.
- **Isovolumetric relaxation.** The ventricles relax, pressure falls, and the semilunar valves snap shut. All valves are again closed until ventricular pressure drops below atrial pressure and filling begins again.

### Key Volumes

The volume of blood in a ventricle at the end of filling is the end-diastolic volume (EDV), typically about 120 mL. The volume left after contraction is the end-systolic volume (ESV), typically about 50 mL. The difference is the stroke volume (SV = EDV − ESV), about 70 mL per beat. The ejection fraction is stroke volume divided by end-diastolic volume; a normal value is roughly 55 to 70 percent. A reduced ejection fraction is one marker used to characterize some types of heart failure.

## Cardiac Output

Cardiac output (CO) is the volume of blood each ventricle pumps per minute. It equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume: CO = HR × SV. With a resting heart rate of 70 beats per minute and a stroke volume of 70 mL, cardiac output is 4,900 mL, or about 4.9 liters per minute. During intense exercise, a trained athlete's cardiac output can rise to 25 to 35 liters per minute.

Stroke volume is influenced by three main factors:

- **Preload**: how much the ventricle is stretched by filling before it contracts. According to the Frank-Starling law of the heart, a ventricle that is filled more contracts more forcefully, within limits. This automatically matches the output of the heart to the amount of blood returning to it.
- **Contractility**: the intrinsic strength of contraction at a given stretch, which is increased by sympathetic nerve stimulation and by hormones such as adrenaline (epinephrine).
- **Afterload**: the resistance the ventricle must overcome to eject blood, largely set by arterial pressure. Higher afterload reduces stroke volume.

Heart rate is adjusted by the autonomic nervous system. Sympathetic nerves speed up the SA node, while the vagus nerve, part of the parasympathetic system, slows it. At rest, vagal tone dominates, which is why the resting heart rate is lower than the SA node's intrinsic firing rate of around 100 beats per minute.

## Blood Pressure

Blood pressure is the force that blood exerts against the walls of the arteries. It is measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and reported as two numbers. Systolic pressure is the peak pressure during ventricular ejection; diastolic pressure is the lowest pressure, reached just before the next beat. A typical healthy reading is around 120/80 mmHg. The difference between the two numbers is the pulse pressure, about 40 mmHg in this example.

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) is the average pressure driving blood into the tissues. Because the heart spends longer in diastole than systole at rest, MAP is closer to diastolic pressure and can be estimated as diastolic pressure plus one third of the pulse pressure. For 120/80, MAP is about 80 + 40/3 ≈ 93 mmHg.

Physiologically, mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output multiplied by total peripheral resistance (MAP = CO × TPR). Resistance is controlled mainly by arterioles, small arteries with muscular walls that can constrict or dilate. Because resistance to flow varies inversely with the fourth power of a vessel's radius, halving the radius of a vessel increases its resistance about sixteenfold.

### Measuring Blood Pressure

Blood pressure is classically measured with a sphygmomanometer: an inflatable cuff around the upper arm and a pressure gauge. The cuff is inflated to stop flow in the brachial artery and then slowly deflated while a clinician listens with a stethoscope. The tapping sounds that appear and then disappear, called Korotkoff sounds, mark systolic and diastolic pressures respectively. Automated cuffs estimate the same values using oscillations in cuff pressure.

### Short-Term and Long-Term Regulation

The body regulates blood pressure on several time scales:

- **Baroreceptor reflex (seconds).** Stretch-sensitive receptors in the carotid sinus and the aortic arch detect changes in pressure and signal the brainstem. If pressure rises, the brainstem slows the heart and dilates vessels; if it falls, as when standing up quickly, it speeds the heart and constricts vessels.
- **Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (minutes to hours).** When the kidneys sense low blood flow, they release renin, which triggers production of angiotensin II, a powerful vasoconstrictor. Angiotensin II also stimulates aldosterone release from the adrenal glands, causing the kidneys to retain sodium and water and increase blood volume.
- **Kidney control of fluid volume (days).** Over the long term, the kidneys adjust how much salt and water is excreted, which is the dominant determinant of long-run blood pressure.

## Hypertension Basics

Hypertension is a condition in which blood pressure remains persistently elevated. Under the 2017 guidelines of the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association, normal blood pressure is below 120/80 mmHg, elevated is a systolic of 120 to 129 with diastolic below 80, stage 1 hypertension is 130 to 139 systolic or 80 to 89 diastolic, and stage 2 hypertension is 140 or higher systolic or 90 or higher diastolic. Other organizations, including some European bodies, use 140/90 as the threshold for diagnosis.

Hypertension is often called a "silent killer" because it usually produces no symptoms for years while it damages organs. Sustained high pressure forces the left ventricle to work harder, causing it to thicken (left ventricular hypertrophy), and damages artery walls, accelerating atherosclerosis. Over time it raises the risk of stroke, heart attack, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and damage to the blood vessels of the retina.

About 90 to 95 percent of cases are classified as primary (essential) hypertension, with no single identifiable cause; contributing factors include genetics, aging, high sodium intake, excess body weight, physical inactivity, and heavy alcohol use. The remaining cases are secondary hypertension, caused by an identifiable condition such as kidney disease or certain hormonal disorders.

Lifestyle measures that are commonly studied for lowering blood pressure include reducing salt intake, regular aerobic exercise, maintaining a healthy weight, limiting alcohol, and eating patterns such as the DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension), which emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy. Medication classes used by clinicians include diuretics, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and calcium channel blockers. Decisions about diagnosis and treatment belong to qualified health professionals.

## Summary

- The heart has four chambers and four one-way valves; the left ventricle has the thickest wall.
- The SA node is the natural pacemaker; the AV node delays the signal so the atria can empty.
- Stroke volume is end-diastolic volume minus end-systolic volume, about 70 mL at rest.
- Cardiac output equals heart rate times stroke volume, about 5 liters per minute at rest.
- Mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance.
- Persistent hypertension silently damages the heart, brain, kidneys, and blood vessels.
