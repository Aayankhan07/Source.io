## Introduction

The Industrial Revolution was the transition from an economy based largely on hand production and agriculture to one in which machines, factories, and inanimate power sources such as coal-fired steam engines played a central role. It began in Great Britain in roughly the 1760s and spread to continental Europe and North America during the nineteenth century. The term itself was popularized by the English economic historian Arnold Toynbee in lectures delivered in the early 1880s, though French writers had used similar language earlier.

Historians debate how "revolutionary" the change really was. Economic growth in Britain was slower than the word suggests: estimates by historians such as Nicholas Crafts put annual growth in national output at under 2 percent for much of the period between 1760 and 1830. Nonetheless, over several generations the cumulative effect transformed how people worked, where they lived, and what they could buy. Between 1750 and 1850, the population of England and Wales roughly tripled, from about 6 million to nearly 18 million, and the country became the first in the world in which more people lived in towns than in the countryside, a milestone recorded in the census of 1851.

## Why Britain? Causes and Preconditions

No single cause explains why industrialization began in Britain. Historians usually point to a combination of factors.

### Agricultural change

During the eighteenth century, British agriculture became markedly more productive. Enclosure, the process of consolidating scattered open-field strips and common land into compact, privately controlled farms, accelerated after Parliament passed hundreds of enclosure acts, especially between about 1760 and 1820. New crop rotations, such as the four-field Norfolk system of wheat, turnips, barley, and clover, reduced the need to leave land fallow and provided winter fodder for livestock. Improvements in selective breeding, associated with Robert Bakewell and his work on sheep and cattle, increased the yield of meat. Greater food output allowed a growing population to be fed with a smaller share of workers on the land, freeing labor for manufacturing.

### Natural resources and geography

Britain had abundant, easily accessible deposits of coal and iron ore, often located close together. No part of the island is more than about 70 miles from the sea, and navigable rivers and an expanding canal network made transporting heavy goods relatively cheap. The Bridgewater Canal, opened in 1761 to carry coal from the Duke of Bridgewater's mines at Worsley to Manchester, is said to have halved the price of coal in that city and set off a wave of canal building often called "canal mania" in the 1790s.

### Capital, institutions, and markets

Britain had a relatively stable political system after 1688, secure property rights, and a patent system that allowed inventors to profit from their ideas. Banks, including a growing number of country banks, and a developed market in government debt made capital available. Overseas trade and colonies provided raw materials such as cotton and sugar and markets for finished goods, though historians disagree about how important colonial profits, including those from the Atlantic slave trade, were to financing industry.

### High wages and cheap energy

One influential explanation, associated with the economic historian Robert Allen, argues that British wages were high relative to the cost of capital and energy. Because labor was expensive and coal was cheap, British businesses had a strong incentive to invent and adopt labor-saving machines powered by coal. The same machines were often unprofitable in countries such as France or India, where wages were lower.

## The Textile Industry

Cotton textiles were the leading sector of early industrialization. Before mechanization, cloth was made in the "putting-out" or domestic system, in which merchants supplied raw materials to families who spun and wove at home. A series of inventions broke the bottlenecks in production and moved the work into factories.

### Key textile inventions

- **The flying shuttle (1733)**, patented by John Kay, allowed a weaver to send the shuttle across the loom with a cord, doubling weaving speed and creating a demand for more yarn than spinners could supply.
- **The spinning jenny (about 1764)**, invented by James Hargreaves, let one worker spin several threads at once, initially eight spindles. It was small enough to be used in a cottage.
- **The water frame (patented 1769)**, developed by Richard Arkwright, used water power to spin strong cotton yarn suitable for the warp threads of cloth. Because it needed a water wheel, it encouraged the construction of large mills.
- **The spinning mule (1779)**, invented by Samuel Crompton, combined features of the jenny and the water frame to produce fine, strong yarn. It became the dominant spinning machine of the nineteenth century.
- **The power loom (1785)**, patented by Edmund Cartwright, mechanized weaving, although it took several decades of improvement before it widely replaced handloom weavers in the 1820s and 1830s.

In the United States, Eli Whitney's cotton gin of 1793 made removing seeds from short-staple cotton far quicker. This greatly expanded cotton production in the American South, worked by enslaved people, and supplied British mills with cheap raw cotton.

### The factory system

Arkwright's mill at Cromford in Derbyshire, opened in 1771, is often described as the first modern factory. It brought hundreds of workers under one roof, ran on shifts, and imposed strict discipline governed by the clock. Arkwright built housing for his workers, creating a factory village. By the 1830s Manchester, nicknamed "Cottonopolis," was the center of a huge cotton district in Lancashire. British raw cotton imports rose from about 2.5 million pounds in 1760 to more than 360 million pounds by 1830.

## Iron and Coal

Iron production was transformed by switching from charcoal to coal-based fuel. In 1709 Abraham Darby I, at Coalbrookdale in Shropshire, successfully smelted iron ore using coke, a purified form of coal. This freed ironmaking from dependence on dwindling forests. Later, in 1784, Henry Cort patented the puddling process, which stirred molten pig iron to remove carbon and produce wrought iron on a large scale, along with a rolling mill that shaped it quickly. The Iron Bridge over the River Severn near Coalbrookdale, completed in 1779, was the first major bridge in the world made of cast iron and became a symbol of the new age.

Coal output in Britain rose from roughly 5 million tons around 1750 to about 50 million tons by 1850. Deeper mines created a pressing problem: flooding. This problem drove the development of the steam engine.

## Steam Power

### Newcomen and Watt

The first commercially successful steam engine was the atmospheric engine of Thomas Newcomen, first installed in 1712 near Dudley to pump water from a coal mine. It worked by condensing steam inside the cylinder to create a partial vacuum, so that atmospheric pressure pushed the piston down. It was reliable but wasted a great deal of fuel because the cylinder was repeatedly heated and cooled.

James Watt, an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow, realized in 1765 that this waste could be avoided by condensing the steam in a separate chamber, keeping the main cylinder hot. He patented the separate condenser in 1769. Watt went into partnership with the Birmingham manufacturer Matthew Boulton, and their firm began producing engines in 1776. Watt engines used roughly a quarter of the coal of a comparable Newcomen engine. In the 1780s Watt added a sun-and-planet gear system that converted the piston's up-and-down motion into rotary motion, so steam could drive factory machinery rather than just pumps. Watt also coined the unit "horsepower" to market his engines, and the SI unit of power, the watt, is named after him.

### High-pressure steam

Watt's engines operated at low pressure. In the early 1800s Richard Trevithick in Cornwall built high-pressure engines that were smaller and lighter, making it possible to mount them on vehicles. In 1804 a Trevithick locomotive hauled ten tons of iron and about seventy passengers along a tramway at the Penydarren ironworks in South Wales, the first time a steam locomotive pulled a load on rails.

The adoption of steam was gradual. As late as 1830, water wheels still supplied a large share of power for British industry, and steam did not overtake water power until around the middle of the century.

## Railways

### Early lines

The Stockton and Darlington Railway, opened in 1825, was the first public railway to use steam locomotives, though horses also pulled some trains. Its engineer was George Stephenson, a self-taught mechanic from Northumberland. The decisive breakthrough came with the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened in 1830, the first intercity line operated entirely by steam locomotives on a fixed timetable, carrying both passengers and freight. In the Rainhill Trials of 1829, held to choose locomotives for this line, the Rocket, designed by George and his son Robert Stephenson, won with a top speed of about 30 miles per hour. The opening day was marred by the death of the politician William Huskisson, who was struck by the Rocket.

### Railway mania and impact

The 1840s saw a speculative boom called "railway mania," when Parliament authorized thousands of miles of new track and investors poured money into railway shares, many of which later collapsed. Even so, by 1850 Britain had over 6,000 miles of railway. Railways cut journey times dramatically: a trip from London to Manchester that had taken more than a day by coach could be made in a few hours. They created demand for iron, coal, and engineering skill, standardized time across the country through "railway time," and allowed fresh food and newspapers to travel nationwide. Isambard Kingdom Brunel's Great Western Railway, using an unusually wide gauge of 7 feet, competed with the 4 feet 8.5 inches "standard gauge" favored by Stephenson; the Gauge Act of 1846 required new lines to use the standard gauge.

## Social Effects

### Urbanization and living conditions

Industrial towns grew with startling speed. Manchester's population rose from about 25,000 in 1772 to over 300,000 by 1850. Housing was often built cheaply and densely, with back-to-back houses sharing walls on three sides, poor ventilation, and few sanitary facilities. Polluted water supplies led to repeated epidemics; cholera struck Britain in 1831-32, 1848-49, and later. Edwin Chadwick's 1842 Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population documented these conditions and helped bring about the Public Health Act of 1848, which created a General Board of Health.

### Work and labor

Factory work imposed long hours, often 12 to 14 hours a day, six days a week, under strict supervision and fines. Children were widely employed in textile mills and mines because they were cheap and could squeeze into small spaces. Pauper apprentices were sometimes sent from workhouses to distant mills. Reform came slowly. The Factory Act of 1833 banned employment of children under 9 in textile mills, limited the hours of children aged 9 to 13, and, importantly, created a paid factory inspectorate to enforce the rules. The Mines Act of 1842 banned women and girls, and boys under 10, from working underground. The Ten Hours Act of 1847 limited women and young people in textile mills to 10 hours a day.

### Protest and organization

Not everyone welcomed machinery. Between 1811 and 1816, the Luddites, named after a mythical leader "Ned Ludd," smashed knitting frames and power looms in the Midlands and the North, protesting against wage cuts and the loss of skilled work. The government deployed thousands of soldiers and made machine-breaking a capital offense. Workers also tried to organize: the Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800 banned trade unions, but they were repealed in 1824. In 1834 six farm laborers from Dorset, known as the Tolpuddle Martyrs, were sentenced to transportation to Australia for swearing an oath to join a union, causing widespread protest. Chartism, a mass political movement of the late 1830s and 1840s, demanded political reforms through the People's Charter of 1838, including votes for all men and the secret ballot.

### Class and living standards

Industrialization created a growing middle class of factory owners, merchants, and professionals, and a large urban working class. The "standard of living debate" among historians asks whether workers' lives improved between about 1780 and 1850. "Optimists" point to rising real wages after about 1820 and cheaper consumer goods, while "pessimists" emphasize poor health, falling average heights in some groups, and harsh working conditions. Many historians now conclude that real wages stagnated until roughly the 1830s and rose more clearly afterward. Contemporary observers such as Friedrich Engels, whose The Condition of the Working Class in England was published in 1845, described the squalor of Manchester in vivid terms.

## Legacy

By 1851, when the Great Exhibition was held in the Crystal Palace in London's Hyde Park, Britain was proudly described as the "workshop of the world." The exhibition attracted about six million visitors and displayed the products of industry from many nations. Industrialization had spread to Belgium, France, the German states, and the United States, and it set the pattern for the sustained economic growth that distinguishes the modern world from earlier eras. It also brought lasting environmental costs, from smoke-blackened cities to the beginning of large-scale fossil-fuel carbon emissions.
