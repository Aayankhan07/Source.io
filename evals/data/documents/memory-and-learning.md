## Introduction

Memory is the capacity to encode, store, and later retrieve information. Psychologists usually describe memory as a sequence of three processes. Encoding is the transformation of incoming information into a form the brain can store. Storage is the maintenance of that information over time. Retrieval is the process of bringing stored information back into awareness or using it to guide behavior. A failure at any one of these stages can produce what we experience as forgetting.

This handout describes the major memory systems, explains what makes encoding strong or weak, reviews research on forgetting, and summarizes the study techniques that the evidence most strongly supports. Classic experiments are described in summary form so that students understand what was tested and what was found.

## The Multi-Store Model

In 1968, Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin proposed an influential framework often called the modal model or multi-store model. It describes three stores through which information flows: sensory memory, short-term memory, and long-term memory. Attention moves information from sensory memory into short-term memory, and rehearsal helps move it from short-term into long-term memory. Although later research has refined the model considerably, it remains a useful starting map.

### Sensory Memory

Sensory memory holds a brief, high-capacity copy of what the senses register. Visual sensory memory is called iconic memory and lasts roughly a quarter to half a second. Auditory sensory memory is called echoic memory and lasts longer, around 2 to 4 seconds, which is why you can sometimes "replay" the last few words someone said even when you were not paying attention.

George Sperling demonstrated the large capacity of iconic memory in 1960. He flashed grids of letters, typically three rows of four letters, for about 50 milliseconds. When asked to report the whole grid, people recalled only about four or five letters. But when a tone sounded right after the display, signaling which single row to report, people could report most of the letters in any cued row. This partial-report technique showed that nearly the whole grid was briefly available, but it faded before people could name it all.

### Short-Term and Working Memory

Short-term memory holds a small amount of information in an active, readily available state. Without rehearsal, its contents fade within about 15 to 30 seconds. In 1959, John Brown in Britain and the husband-and-wife team Lloyd and Margaret Peterson in the United States showed that people forgot three-letter consonant strings rapidly when prevented from rehearsing by counting backward by threes; after 18 seconds of counting, recall dropped to around 10 percent.

The capacity of short-term memory is limited. In a famous 1956 paper, George Miller argued that people can hold about seven, plus or minus two, items. Later researchers, notably Nelson Cowan, argued that the true limit is closer to about four meaningful units when rehearsal and grouping are prevented. Capacity can be stretched through chunking, the grouping of separate elements into larger meaningful units. A string like F-B-I-C-I-A-U-S-A is hard to hold as nine letters but easy as three familiar acronyms.

In 1974, Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch proposed that short-term memory is better understood as working memory, an active workspace with several components:

- The **phonological loop** holds speech-based and sound information, sustained by inner rehearsal.
- The **visuospatial sketchpad** holds visual images and spatial layouts.
- The **central executive** directs attention and coordinates the other components.
- The **episodic buffer**, added by Baddeley in 2000, integrates information across components and links it with long-term memory.

Evidence for separate components comes from dual-task studies: people can usually rehearse a list of words while tracing a shape, but performance suffers when two verbal tasks or two spatial tasks must be done at once.

### Long-Term Memory

Long-term memory has an essentially unlimited capacity and can retain information for a lifetime. It is commonly divided into two broad types.

**Explicit (declarative) memory** is memory that can be consciously recalled and described. Endel Tulving in 1972 distinguished two forms: episodic memory, for personally experienced events tied to a time and place, such as your last birthday; and semantic memory, for general facts and knowledge, such as the capital of France.

**Implicit (nondeclarative) memory** influences behavior without conscious recollection. It includes procedural memory for skills such as riding a bicycle or typing, priming (where earlier exposure to a stimulus makes later processing of it faster), and classical conditioning.

### The Case of H.M.

Much of what is known about the brain basis of memory comes from the patient Henry Molaison, known in research for decades by his initials H.M. In 1953, to treat severe epilepsy, surgeons removed much of the medial temporal lobe on both sides of his brain, including most of the hippocampus. Afterward he could no longer form new long-term explicit memories, a condition called anterograde amnesia, although his short-term memory and intelligence were intact. Remarkably, when Brenda Milner had him practice mirror drawing, tracing a star while watching only its reflection, he improved over days while having no memory of ever doing the task. His case showed that the hippocampus is essential for forming new declarative memories but not for procedural learning, and that memory is not a single system.

The serial position effect offers further evidence for separate stores. When people recall a list of words in any order, they remember the first few items (the primacy effect) and the last few items (the recency effect) best. The recency effect disappears if recall is delayed by about 30 seconds of distracting activity, suggesting it relies on short-term memory, while the primacy effect reflects extra rehearsal that moves early items into long-term memory.

## Encoding: Getting Information In

How information is processed at the time of learning strongly determines whether it will be remembered.

### Levels of Processing

In 1972, Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart proposed the levels-of-processing framework, arguing that deeper, meaning-based processing produces more durable memories than shallow processing. In a classic follow-up by Craik and Endel Tulving in 1975, participants answered questions about words that required attention to their typeface (shallow), their rhyme (intermediate), or whether they fit into a sentence (deep). In a surprise memory test, words processed for meaning were remembered far better than words judged only for their letter case.

### Elaboration, Self-Reference, and Imagery

Several related strategies strengthen encoding:

- **Elaboration**: connecting new information to existing knowledge, such as explaining why a fact is true.
- **Self-reference effect**: information related to oneself is remembered especially well.
- **Dual coding**: Allan Paivio proposed that concrete words are remembered better because they can be stored both verbally and as images.
- **Mnemonics**: the method of loci, dating back to ancient Greek and Roman orators, links items to be remembered with locations along a familiar route.

### Context and State

Memory is better when the context at retrieval matches the context at encoding, an idea Tulving called the encoding specificity principle. In a well-known 1975 study, Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley had scuba divers learn word lists either on land or about 6 meters underwater. Divers recalled about 40 percent more words when tested in the same environment where they had learned them.

## Forgetting

### The Forgetting Curve

The first systematic experiments on memory were conducted by the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus, who published his results in 1885. Using himself as the only subject, he memorized lists of nonsense syllables, such as consonant-vowel-consonant strings like "DAX" or "ZOF," chosen to avoid prior associations. He measured retention using the savings method: how much less time it took to relearn a list compared with learning it the first time.

Ebbinghaus found that forgetting is rapid at first and then levels off. In his data, he had lost more than half of the savings within the first hour, and after about a day roughly two thirds was gone; after that, the decline became much slower, with some savings still detectable after a month. This steep-then-flat shape is called the forgetting curve. Later replications, including a 2015 study by Jaap Murre and Joeri Dros, produced closely similar curves.

### Why We Forget

Several explanations of forgetting have been proposed:

- **Decay**: memory traces fade over time if they are not used. Pure decay is hard to test because time always passes alongside other events.
- **Interference**: other learning disrupts memory. In proactive interference, older learning makes it harder to recall new information, such as writing last year's date in January. In retroactive interference, new learning disrupts recall of older information, such as forgetting an old phone number after learning a new one. A classic 1924 study by John Jenkins and Karl Dallenbach found that people who slept after learning nonsense syllables remembered more than those who stayed awake, consistent with interference from waking activity.
- **Retrieval failure**: the information is stored but cannot be accessed without the right cue. The tip-of-the-tongue state, studied by Roger Brown and David McNeill in 1966, is an everyday example: people often know the first letter or number of syllables of a word they cannot quite produce.
- **Consolidation failure**: new memories need time to stabilize. Disruptions such as head injury can prevent consolidation, producing retrograde amnesia for events just before the injury.

### Memory Is Reconstructive

Retrieval is not like replaying a recording. In the 1930s, Frederic Bartlett asked English participants to retell a Native American folk tale, "The War of the Ghosts," and found that over repeated retellings they shortened it and altered unfamiliar details to fit their own cultural expectations, which he described in terms of schemas. Later, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed in 1974 that the wording of a question could alter eyewitness memory: participants who were asked how fast cars were going when they "smashed" into each other gave higher speed estimates, and a week later were more likely to falsely report seeing broken glass, than those asked about cars that "hit" each other.

## Evidence-Based Learning Strategies

Research on memory has direct implications for how students should study. In a 2013 review, John Dunlosky and colleagues evaluated ten common study techniques and rated two as having high utility: practice testing and distributed practice. Popular techniques such as highlighting and rereading were rated as having low utility.

### Retrieval Practice (the Testing Effect)

Actively recalling information strengthens memory more than passively reviewing it, a finding called the testing effect. In a 2006 study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students study prose passages and then either restudy them or take a free-recall test. On a test five minutes later, restudying looked slightly better. But on a test one week later, students who had practiced recall remembered substantially more, about 56 percent compared with about 42 percent for restudying. Notably, students who restudied predicted they would do better, showing that feelings of fluency can mislead learners.

Practical forms of retrieval practice include flashcards, self-quizzing, practice exams, and closing the book to write down everything one remembers.

### Spaced Practice (the Spacing Effect)

Spreading study sessions out over time produces better long-term retention than cramming the same amount of study into one session. Ebbinghaus himself noticed this effect. A large 2008 study by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues, with more than 1,300 participants, found that the ideal gap between study sessions depends on how long the information must be remembered: for a test about a week away, a gap of a day or two worked well, while for a test a year away, gaps of several weeks were better. A common rule of thumb drawn from this work is to space reviews at roughly 10 to 20 percent of the desired retention interval.

Spaced repetition software builds on this principle. The SuperMemo algorithm, developed by Piotr Woźniak in the late 1980s, and the Leitner box system, devised by Sebastian Leitner in the 1970s, schedule each item to reappear just before it is likely to be forgotten, lengthening the interval each time it is recalled successfully.

### Interleaving and Desirable Difficulties

Interleaving means mixing different types of problems within a study session rather than practicing one type in a block. In a 2007 study by Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor, students practicing volume problems for different geometric solids performed worse during interleaved practice but scored about three times higher on a later test than students who practiced in blocks.

Robert Bjork coined the term "desirable difficulties" for conditions such as spacing, testing, and interleaving that make learning feel harder and slower in the moment but produce stronger, more flexible long-term retention.

### Sleep

Sleep supports consolidation. During slow-wave sleep, the hippocampus is thought to replay recent experiences, helping transfer them into long-term cortical storage. Pulling an all-nighter before an exam therefore undermines both encoding of new material and consolidation of what was already studied.

## Summary

- Memory involves encoding, storage, and retrieval.
- Sensory memory is brief; short-term memory holds about four to seven items for under 30 seconds without rehearsal.
- The hippocampus is critical for forming new declarative memories, as the case of H.M. showed.
- Deep, meaningful processing produces stronger memories than shallow processing.
- Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve shows rapid early forgetting that then levels off.
- Retrieval practice and spaced practice are among the most effective study strategies.
