## 1. Reversible Reactions

Many chemical reactions do not run to completion. Instead, as products form, they begin reacting with one another to regenerate the reactants. A reaction that can proceed in both directions is called a reversible reaction and is written with a double arrow (⇌). For example, when colorless dinitrogen tetroxide gas (N2O4) is sealed in a flask, some of it decomposes into brown nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and at the same time NO2 molecules combine to re-form N2O4:

N2O4(g) ⇌ 2 NO2(g)

The reaction written left to right is called the forward reaction, and the one written right to left is called the reverse reaction. In a closed system, any reversible reaction will eventually reach a state in which the composition of the mixture stops changing. That state is chemical equilibrium.

## 2. Dynamic Equilibrium

At the start of a reaction, only reactants are present, so the forward rate is high and the reverse rate is zero. As reactants are consumed, the forward rate slows; as products accumulate, the reverse rate speeds up. Eventually the two rates become equal. From that moment the concentrations of all species remain constant, even though both reactions continue to occur at the molecular level.

This is why chemical equilibrium is described as **dynamic** rather than static. Molecules are still reacting, but every forward event is balanced by a reverse event, so there is no net change. Experiments with radioactive isotopes demonstrate this: if a small amount of a labeled reactant is added to a mixture already at equilibrium, the label soon appears in the products, proving that exchange continues even though overall concentrations do not change.

Several features characterize a system at equilibrium:

- It must be a **closed system**, so that no substances enter or leave.
- The rates of the forward and reverse reactions are **equal**.
- Concentrations of reactants and products are **constant**, but generally **not equal** to each other.
- Macroscopic properties such as color, pressure, and density no longer change.
- Equilibrium can be approached from either direction: starting with pure reactants or pure products leads to the same equilibrium state at a given temperature, provided the total amounts of each element are the same.

A useful everyday analogy is a person walking up a down-moving escalator at exactly the speed the escalator descends. The walker is constantly moving, yet stays at the same height.

## 3. The Equilibrium Constant, K

In 1864 the Norwegian chemists Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage proposed the law of mass action, which states that at equilibrium a particular ratio of product and reactant concentrations has a constant value at a given temperature. For a general reaction

a A + b B ⇌ c C + d D

the equilibrium constant expression in terms of concentration is:

Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / ([A]^a [B]^b)

Square brackets indicate molar concentrations (mol/L) at equilibrium, and each concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation. Products appear in the numerator and reactants in the denominator.

### 3.1 Rules for writing K

- Pure solids and pure liquids are **left out** of the expression because their concentrations are effectively constant. For the decomposition of calcium carbonate, CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO2(g), the expression is simply Kc = [CO2].
- Water is omitted when it acts as the solvent in dilute aqueous solutions.
- For gas-phase reactions, the constant can also be written in terms of partial pressures, called Kp. The two are related by Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn, where Δn is the moles of gaseous products minus the moles of gaseous reactants, R is the gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature in kelvin.
- If a reaction is reversed, the new constant is the reciprocal of the original, 1/K.
- If the coefficients of a reaction are multiplied by a factor n, the new constant is K raised to the power n.
- If two reactions are added together, the constant for the overall reaction is the product of the individual constants.

### 3.2 What the size of K tells us

The magnitude of K indicates the position of equilibrium:

- **K much greater than 1** (for example, larger than about 10^3): products dominate at equilibrium, and the reaction lies far to the right.
- **K much less than 1** (for example, smaller than about 10^−3): reactants dominate, and very little product forms.
- **K close to 1:** significant amounts of both reactants and products are present.

The value of K says nothing about how fast equilibrium is reached. A reaction can have an enormous K yet proceed so slowly that it is not observed at room temperature. The conversion of diamond to graphite is thermodynamically favored but kinetically so slow that diamonds persist for geological time.

### 3.3 K depends only on temperature

For a given reaction, K changes only when temperature changes. Adding reactants, removing products, changing pressure, or adding a catalyst all leave K unchanged, even though they may change the individual equilibrium concentrations.

## 4. The Reaction Quotient, Q

The reaction quotient Q has the same mathematical form as K, but it uses the concentrations present at any moment, not necessarily at equilibrium. Comparing Q with K predicts the direction in which a reaction will proceed:

- **Q < K:** the ratio of products to reactants is too small. The reaction proceeds in the **forward** direction, forming more products, until Q rises to equal K.
- **Q > K:** there are too many products relative to reactants. The reaction proceeds in the **reverse** direction until Q falls to equal K.
- **Q = K:** the system is already at equilibrium and there is no net change.

### 4.1 Worked example using Q

Consider the reaction H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2 HI(g), for which Kc is about 50 at 445 °C. Suppose a 1.0 L flask at that temperature contains 0.10 mol H2, 0.10 mol I2, and 0.50 mol HI.

Q = [HI]^2 / ([H2][I2]) = (0.50)^2 / (0.10 × 0.10) = 0.25 / 0.010 = 25

Since Q = 25 is less than K = 50, the mixture is not at equilibrium. The reaction will proceed in the forward direction, consuming some H2 and I2 and producing more HI until the ratio reaches 50.

### 4.2 Calculating equilibrium concentrations

Equilibrium problems are often solved with an ICE table, which lists the Initial concentrations, the Change in each concentration (expressed with a variable x and the stoichiometric coefficients), and the Equilibrium concentrations. The equilibrium expressions are substituted into the K expression and solved for x. When K is very small, the approximation that x is negligible compared with the initial concentration often simplifies the algebra; a common check is that x should be less than 5% of the initial value.

## 5. Le Chatelier's Principle

In 1884 the French chemist Henri Le Chatelier stated a general rule for predicting how an equilibrium responds to a disturbance: if a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in conditions, the position of equilibrium shifts in the direction that partially counteracts the change. The principle is qualitative, but it can always be explained in terms of how the disturbance makes Q differ from K, or, for temperature, how it changes K itself.

### 5.1 Changes in concentration

Adding a reactant or removing a product makes Q smaller than K, so the equilibrium shifts to the right, forming more products. Adding a product or removing a reactant shifts the equilibrium to the left. A familiar laboratory illustration uses the iron(III) thiocyanate equilibrium: adding more thiocyanate ions deepens the blood-red color of the solution as more of the colored complex forms.

### 5.2 Changes in pressure and volume

For reactions involving gases, decreasing the volume of the container (which increases the total pressure) shifts the equilibrium toward the side with **fewer moles of gas**. Increasing the volume shifts it toward the side with more moles of gas. If both sides have the same number of gas moles, as in H2 + I2 ⇌ 2 HI, a pressure change has no effect on the position of equilibrium. Adding an inert gas such as argon at constant volume increases the total pressure but does not change any partial pressures, so it does not shift the equilibrium.

### 5.3 Changes in temperature

Temperature is the only change that alters the value of K. The trick is to treat heat as if it were a reactant or a product:

- For an **exothermic** reaction (ΔH negative), heat is a product. Raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium to the left and **decreases** K.
- For an **endothermic** reaction (ΔH positive), heat is a reactant. Raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium to the right and **increases** K.

The N2O4 ⇌ 2 NO2 system demonstrates this vividly. The forward reaction is endothermic, so a sealed tube of the gas mixture turns darker brown when placed in hot water and paler when placed in ice water.

### 5.4 Catalysts

A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions by exactly the same factor by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy. It therefore helps a system reach equilibrium sooner but has **no effect** on the position of equilibrium or on the value of K.

## 6. Industrial Application: The Haber Process

The most famous industrial application of equilibrium principles is the Haber process (also called the Haber-Bosch process), which synthesizes ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen:

N2(g) + 3 H2(g) ⇌ 2 NH3(g)    ΔH ≈ −92 kJ per mole of reaction

The German chemist Fritz Haber demonstrated the reaction on a laboratory scale in 1909, and Carl Bosch, an engineer at the chemical company BASF, scaled it up to an industrial plant that began operating at Oppau in 1913. Haber received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1918, and Bosch shared the 1931 prize for developing high-pressure chemical methods. Ammonia is the starting material for most synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, and the process is often credited with enabling the food supply that supports a large fraction of today's world population. It is estimated to consume roughly 1 to 2 percent of the world's energy output.

### 6.1 Applying Le Chatelier's principle

The choice of operating conditions is a compromise between equilibrium yield, reaction rate, and cost.

**Pressure.** The reactant side has 4 moles of gas (1 N2 + 3 H2) and the product side has 2 moles. High pressure therefore shifts the equilibrium toward ammonia. Plants typically operate at around 150 to 300 atmospheres (roughly 15 to 30 MPa). Even higher pressures would raise the yield further, but the cost of thick-walled steel vessels, powerful compressors, and safety measures makes them uneconomic.

**Temperature.** Because the forward reaction is exothermic, low temperatures favor a higher equilibrium yield of ammonia. However, at low temperatures the reaction is far too slow, because the strong triple bond in N2 is difficult to break. Industry therefore uses a compromise temperature of about 400 to 450 °C, which gives an acceptable rate even though the equilibrium yield is lower than it would be at room temperature.

**Catalyst.** An iron-based catalyst, promoted with small amounts of potassium oxide and aluminum oxide, increases the rate of reaching equilibrium. It does not increase the equilibrium yield, but it makes the moderate operating temperature practical.

**Removing product.** The gas mixture leaving the reactor is cooled so that ammonia, which has a much higher boiling point than nitrogen or hydrogen, condenses into a liquid and is removed. Removing a product keeps pulling the system forward. The unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled back into the reactor.

### 6.2 Yield in practice

Under typical conditions, only about 15 percent of the reactants are converted to ammonia in a single pass through the reactor. Because the unreacted gases are continually recycled, the overall conversion eventually reaches about 97 percent. This illustrates a key idea in industrial chemistry: a modest equilibrium yield per pass can still produce an efficient process if the rate is fast and unused reactants are recovered.

## 7. Equilibrium Beyond the Gas Phase

The same principles govern many other systems. Acid dissociation constants (Ka) describe how far weak acids ionize in water; acetic acid has a Ka of about 1.8 × 10^−5, showing that most of its molecules remain un-ionized. Solubility product constants (Ksp) describe sparingly soluble salts, and the common-ion effect, a direct application of Le Chatelier's principle, explains why silver chloride is even less soluble in a solution that already contains chloride ions. In the human body, the binding of oxygen to hemoglobin is an equilibrium that shifts toward oxygen release in tissues where oxygen concentration is low.

## 8. Summary

Chemical equilibrium is a dynamic state in a closed system in which the forward and reverse reaction rates are equal and concentrations remain constant. The equilibrium constant K, defined by the law of mass action, expresses the ratio of products to reactants at equilibrium and depends only on temperature. The reaction quotient Q, compared with K, predicts the direction of net change. Le Chatelier's principle predicts how equilibria respond to changes in concentration, pressure, and temperature, while catalysts affect only the rate. The Haber process shows these ideas working together in one of the most important industrial reactions in history.
