# Stellar Evolution: The Life Cycles of Stars

Stars are not eternal. Each one is born from a cloud of gas and dust, spends most of its life steadily fusing hydrogen, and eventually dies in a way that depends almost entirely on how much mass it started with. This handout follows that life story from beginning to end, introducing the physical processes and the vocabulary astronomers use to describe them.

## Star Formation

### Molecular Clouds

Stars form inside giant molecular clouds, cold and dense regions of the interstellar medium composed mostly of molecular hydrogen (H2) with helium and small amounts of dust. These clouds are extremely cold, typically around 10 to 20 kelvin, and can contain enough material to make hundreds of thousands of stars. The Orion Nebula, about 1,350 light-years from Earth, is the nearest large region of active star formation and is visible to the naked eye as a fuzzy patch in Orion's sword.

A cloud remains stable as long as its internal gas pressure balances the inward pull of its own gravity. If a region of the cloud becomes dense enough, or massive enough, gravity wins and the region begins to collapse. The threshold mass above which collapse occurs is called the Jeans mass, after the British physicist James Jeans. Collapse can be triggered by external events such as the shock wave from a nearby supernova, the collision of two clouds, or the passage of a spiral arm of the galaxy.

### Protostars

As a fragment of cloud collapses, it heats up because gravitational potential energy is converted into thermal energy. Conservation of angular momentum makes the collapsing material spin faster and flatten into a rotating disk, with a dense, hot core at the center called a protostar. The surrounding disk, known as a protoplanetary disk, is where planets may later form. Protostars are often hidden from optical telescopes by the dust around them, so astronomers study them mainly with infrared and radio observations.

Young stars that have not yet begun hydrogen fusion in their cores are called pre-main-sequence stars. Sun-like examples are known as T Tauri stars, named after a variable star in the constellation Taurus. A pre-main-sequence star continues to contract slowly over millions of years. For a star of the Sun's mass, this contraction phase lasts roughly 30 to 50 million years.

### The Minimum Mass for a Star

A true star must become hot enough in its core, about 10 million kelvin, to fuse hydrogen. Objects with less than roughly 0.08 solar masses, about 80 times the mass of Jupiter, never reach this temperature. These failed stars are called brown dwarfs. They glow faintly from leftover heat of contraction and may briefly fuse deuterium, but they never sustain ordinary hydrogen fusion.

## The Main Sequence

### Hydrostatic Equilibrium

Once hydrogen fusion begins in the core, a star settles onto the main sequence, the longest and most stable phase of its life. During this phase the star is in hydrostatic equilibrium: the outward pressure of hot gas, supported by energy released from fusion, exactly balances the inward pull of gravity at every layer. If fusion speeds up slightly, the core expands and cools, slowing fusion again; if fusion slows, the core contracts and heats up. This self-regulating behavior is like a thermostat, and it keeps main-sequence stars remarkably steady for long periods.

### Nuclear Fusion in Stars

Fusion combines light nuclei into heavier ones. Four hydrogen nuclei (protons) are ultimately converted into one helium-4 nucleus. The helium nucleus has about 0.7 percent less mass than the four protons that formed it, and this missing mass is released as energy according to Einstein's relation E = mc². The Sun converts roughly 4 million tonnes of mass into energy every second.

There are two main routes for hydrogen fusion. In the proton-proton chain, which dominates in stars of about the Sun's mass or less, protons fuse directly in a series of steps that produce deuterium, then helium-3, and finally helium-4, along with positrons, neutrinos, and gamma rays. In the CNO cycle, which dominates in stars more massive than about 1.3 solar masses, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen nuclei act as catalysts: they absorb protons and are transformed through a loop of reactions that ends by releasing a helium nucleus and restoring the original carbon. The CNO cycle is extremely sensitive to temperature, which is why it takes over in hotter, more massive cores.

### Mass and Lifetime

Mass is the single most important property of a star. More massive stars have higher core temperatures and pressures, so they fuse hydrogen far faster. Luminosity rises steeply with mass; for main-sequence stars it scales roughly as mass raised to the power 3.5. Because a star's fuel supply is proportional to its mass but its burning rate rises so steeply, more massive stars live much shorter lives. The Sun has a main-sequence lifetime of about 10 billion years and is roughly 4.6 billion years old. A star of 10 solar masses lasts only about 20 to 30 million years, while a red dwarf of 0.1 solar masses could shine for trillions of years, far longer than the current age of the universe, about 13.8 billion years.

## The Hertzsprung-Russell Diagram

### Reading the Diagram

The Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram, developed independently around 1910 by Ejnar Hertzsprung and Henry Norris Russell, is the most important chart in stellar astronomy. It plots luminosity (or absolute magnitude) on the vertical axis against surface temperature (or spectral type, or color) on the horizontal axis. By tradition, temperature increases toward the left, so hot blue stars appear on the left and cool red stars on the right.

Stars are not scattered randomly on the diagram. About 90 percent of stars lie along a diagonal band called the main sequence, running from hot, bright stars at the upper left to cool, dim stars at the lower right. Red giants and supergiants occupy the upper right: they are cool but very luminous because they are enormous. White dwarfs occupy the lower left: they are hot but dim because they are tiny, roughly the size of Earth.

### Spectral Classes

Astronomers classify stars by their spectra using the sequence O, B, A, F, G, K, M, from hottest to coolest. O stars have surface temperatures above about 30,000 kelvin and appear blue, while M stars are below about 3,500 kelvin and appear red. The Sun is a G2 star with a surface temperature of about 5,800 kelvin. A luminosity class, written as a Roman numeral, is often added: V indicates a main-sequence dwarf and III a giant, so the Sun's full classification is G2V.

## Evolution of Low- and Intermediate-Mass Stars

### Becoming a Red Giant

When a Sun-like star has converted the hydrogen in its core to helium, fusion in the core stops. The inert helium core contracts under gravity and heats up, and hydrogen begins fusing in a shell surrounding it. This shell burning produces more energy than core burning did, and the star's outer envelope swells enormously and cools, turning the star into a red giant. When the Sun reaches this stage in about 5 billion years, it will expand to perhaps 100 to 200 times its current radius, likely engulfing Mercury and Venus and possibly Earth.

### The Helium Flash and Helium Burning

The contracting helium core eventually reaches about 100 million kelvin, hot enough for helium fusion. In this process, called the triple-alpha process, three helium-4 nuclei combine to form one carbon-12 nucleus; some carbon then captures another helium nucleus to form oxygen-16. In stars below about 2 solar masses, the core is so dense that it is supported by electron degeneracy pressure rather than ordinary thermal pressure, and helium ignites explosively in an event called the helium flash. The star then settles into a stable phase of core helium burning, sometimes called the horizontal branch for older, metal-poor stars.

### Planetary Nebulae and White Dwarfs

After the core helium is exhausted, the star develops a carbon-oxygen core surrounded by helium- and hydrogen-burning shells and climbs the asymptotic giant branch. Strong stellar winds and pulsations gradually eject its outer layers into space. The expanding shell of gas, lit up by ultraviolet light from the exposed hot core, forms a planetary nebula. The Ring Nebula in Lyra is a well-known example. Planetary nebulae last only about 10,000 to 50,000 years before dispersing.

The exposed core that remains is a white dwarf. A typical white dwarf contains about 0.6 solar masses packed into a sphere roughly the size of Earth, so a teaspoon of its material would weigh several tonnes on Earth. No fusion occurs in a white dwarf; it is supported against gravity by electron degeneracy pressure, a quantum effect arising from the Pauli exclusion principle, which forbids electrons from crowding into the same state. Indian-American astrophysicist Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar showed that electron degeneracy pressure can support a white dwarf only up to about 1.4 solar masses, a limit now called the Chandrasekhar limit. Over billions of years, a white dwarf slowly cools and dims; a fully cooled remnant, called a black dwarf, is theoretical because the universe is not yet old enough for any to exist.

## Evolution of Massive Stars

### Building Heavy Elements

Stars that begin with more than about 8 solar masses follow a more dramatic path. After hydrogen and helium burning, their cores become hot enough to fuse carbon, then neon, oxygen, and silicon, each stage proceeding faster than the one before. In a star of around 25 solar masses, hydrogen burning lasts several million years, but silicon burning lasts only about a day. The result is an onion-like structure of nested shells, each fusing a different element, surrounding a growing core of iron. These stars swell into red supergiants such as Betelgeuse in Orion or Antares in Scorpius.

### Why Iron Ends Fusion

Iron-56 has one of the highest binding energies per nucleon of any nucleus. Fusing elements lighter than iron releases energy, but fusing iron into heavier elements absorbs energy instead. Once the core is mostly iron, the star has no further fuel to resist gravity.

### Core-Collapse Supernovae

When the iron core exceeds the Chandrasekhar limit, electron degeneracy pressure fails. The core collapses in less than a second, with its outer parts falling inward at up to a quarter of the speed of light. Electrons are forced into protons to form neutrons and a flood of neutrinos. When the core reaches nuclear density, it stiffens and rebounds, and the resulting shock wave, helped by the enormous neutrino flux, blasts the star's outer layers into space. This is a Type II, or core-collapse, supernova. For a few weeks it can outshine an entire galaxy of billions of stars. Supernova 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud was the nearest observed supernova in centuries, and detectors on Earth recorded about two dozen neutrinos from it, confirming the core-collapse model.

A different kind of explosion, the Type Ia supernova, occurs when a white dwarf in a binary system gains matter from a companion until it nears the Chandrasekhar limit and undergoes runaway carbon fusion that destroys it completely. Because Type Ia supernovae reach a fairly consistent peak brightness, they serve as standard candles for measuring cosmic distances, and observations of them in the late 1990s revealed that the expansion of the universe is accelerating.

Supernovae are also the universe's chemical factories. Elements heavier than iron, such as gold, silver, and uranium, are formed by rapid neutron capture in supernovae and in mergers of neutron stars. The calcium in our bones and the iron in our blood were produced inside stars that lived and died before the Sun formed.

## Stellar Remnants

### Neutron Stars

If the collapsed core left behind by a supernova has between about 1.4 and roughly 2 to 3 solar masses, it becomes a neutron star, supported by neutron degeneracy pressure and nuclear forces. A neutron star packs more mass than the Sun into a sphere only about 20 kilometers across, giving it a density comparable to that of an atomic nucleus. Neutron stars spin rapidly because the collapse conserved angular momentum, and they have extraordinarily strong magnetic fields.

Some neutron stars are observed as pulsars, which emit beams of radio waves from their magnetic poles. As the star rotates, the beams sweep past Earth like a lighthouse, producing regular pulses. The first pulsar was discovered in 1967 by Jocelyn Bell Burnell, then a graduate student at Cambridge, who noticed a signal repeating every 1.33 seconds. The Crab Pulsar, at the center of the Crab Nebula, is the remnant of a supernova recorded by Chinese astronomers in 1054 and spins about 30 times per second.

### Black Holes

If the remnant core exceeds the maximum mass a neutron star can support, no known force can halt its collapse, and it becomes a black hole, a region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape. The boundary of a black hole is the event horizon, and its radius, called the Schwarzschild radius, is about 3 kilometers for every solar mass. A black hole of 10 solar masses would therefore have an event horizon about 30 kilometers in radius.

Black holes cannot be seen directly, but they reveal themselves through their effects on nearby matter. Cygnus X-1, discovered as a strong X-ray source in the 1960s, was the first object widely accepted as a black hole; gas pulled from its companion star heats to millions of degrees in an accretion disk and emits X-rays. In 2015, the LIGO observatory detected gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime, produced by the merger of two black holes roughly 1.3 billion light-years away, opening an entirely new way to study these objects.

## Summary: Mass Determines Fate

The fate of a star can be summarized by its initial mass. Objects below about 0.08 solar masses become brown dwarfs and never fuse hydrogen. Stars from about 0.08 to 8 solar masses end as white dwarfs, usually after a red giant phase and a planetary nebula. Stars above about 8 solar masses explode as core-collapse supernovae and leave behind neutron stars or, for the most massive progenitors, black holes. In every case, the material a star returns to space enriches the next generation of stars and planets.
