# Cellular Respiration: How Cells Harvest Energy from Food

## Overview

Cellular respiration is the set of metabolic pathways that cells use to break down organic fuel molecules, most commonly glucose, and capture the released energy in the form of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). ATP is the cell's short-term energy currency. When the bond between its second and third phosphate groups is hydrolyzed, roughly 30.5 kilojoules per mole of energy is released under standard conditions, and this energy powers muscle contraction, active transport, biosynthesis, and countless other processes.

The overall chemical summary of aerobic respiration of glucose is:

C6H12O6 + 6 O2 -> 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + energy (as ATP and heat)

Although this equation looks like simple combustion, the cell does not burn glucose in a single step. Instead, it oxidizes glucose gradually through dozens of enzyme-catalyzed reactions, so that energy is released in small, manageable packets. Burning glucose all at once would release about 2,870 kJ per mole as heat, which a cell could not capture efficiently.

Cellular respiration is usually divided into four stages:

1. Glycolysis, which takes place in the cytoplasm.
2. Pyruvate oxidation (sometimes called the link reaction), which takes place in the mitochondrial matrix.
3. The citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle, which takes place in the mitochondrial matrix.
4. Oxidative phosphorylation, which consists of the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis, located in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

## Redox Reactions and Electron Carriers

Respiration is fundamentally a series of oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions. Oxidation is the loss of electrons, and reduction is the gain of electrons. A helpful memory aid is "OIL RIG": oxidation is loss, reduction is gain. Glucose is oxidized to carbon dioxide, and oxygen is reduced to water.

Electrons removed from glucose are not passed directly to oxygen. They are first transferred, together with hydrogen ions, to coenzymes that act as electron shuttles. The two most important carriers are:

- **NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide)**, which is reduced to NADH. NAD+ is derived from the vitamin niacin (vitamin B3).
- **FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide)**, which is reduced to FADH2. FAD is derived from riboflavin (vitamin B2).

These reduced carriers deliver their high-energy electrons to the electron transport chain, where most ATP is ultimately made. Because a cell contains only a limited pool of NAD+, regenerating NAD+ from NADH is essential for glycolysis to continue, a point that becomes important when oxygen is scarce.

## Stage 1: Glycolysis

Glycolysis, meaning "sugar splitting," is a ten-step pathway that converts one six-carbon glucose molecule into two three-carbon molecules of pyruvate. It occurs in the cytosol and does not require oxygen, which is why it is considered one of the most ancient metabolic pathways. Nearly all living organisms, from bacteria to humans, carry out glycolysis.

### The energy investment phase

The first five steps make up the energy investment phase. The cell spends 2 ATP to phosphorylate glucose and its derivatives. The first step is catalyzed by hexokinase, which adds a phosphate to glucose to form glucose-6-phosphate. This phosphorylation traps glucose inside the cell because the charged molecule cannot cross the plasma membrane through glucose transporters.

The third step, catalyzed by phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1), is the key regulatory point of glycolysis. PFK-1 converts fructose-6-phosphate into fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. This enzyme is allosterically inhibited by high levels of ATP and citrate and is activated by AMP. In this way, the cell slows glycolysis when energy is plentiful and speeds it up when energy is in short supply. The six-carbon sugar is then split into two three-carbon molecules, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate, which is converted into a second G3P.

### The energy payoff phase

In the second five steps, each G3P is oxidized and phosphorylated, and ATP is produced by substrate-level phosphorylation, meaning an enzyme transfers a phosphate group directly from a substrate molecule to ADP. Because two G3P molecules pass through the payoff phase, a total of 4 ATP and 2 NADH are produced. The final enzyme, pyruvate kinase, produces pyruvate and ATP.

### Net yield of glycolysis

For each molecule of glucose, glycolysis produces a net gain of 2 ATP, 2 NADH, and 2 pyruvate. The net figure accounts for the 2 ATP invested at the start. Only about a quarter of the chemical energy in glucose is released in glycolysis; most of it remains stored in the two pyruvate molecules.

## Stage 2: Pyruvate Oxidation

When oxygen is available, pyruvate is actively transported from the cytosol into the mitochondrial matrix. There, a large multienzyme assembly called the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex converts each pyruvate into acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA). This conversion involves three changes:

1. A carboxyl group is removed and released as CO2 (decarboxylation).
2. The remaining two-carbon fragment is oxidized, and the electrons reduce NAD+ to NADH.
3. Coenzyme A, a sulfur-containing compound derived from vitamin B5 (pantothenic acid), is attached to form acetyl-CoA.

Because glycolysis produces two pyruvates, this stage yields 2 acetyl-CoA, 2 NADH, and 2 CO2 per glucose. The pyruvate dehydrogenase step is irreversible in animals, which is why animals cannot convert fatty acids (which are broken down to acetyl-CoA) back into glucose in a net way.

## Stage 3: The Citric Acid (Krebs) Cycle

The citric acid cycle was worked out by the biochemist Hans Krebs in 1937, and he received a share of the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1953 for this work. It is also called the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle because its first product, citrate, has three carboxyl groups.

### Steps of the cycle

The cycle has eight steps, each catalyzed by a specific enzyme in the mitochondrial matrix:

1. Acetyl-CoA (2 carbons) combines with oxaloacetate (4 carbons) to form citrate (6 carbons), catalyzed by citrate synthase.
2. Citrate is rearranged into its isomer isocitrate.
3. Isocitrate is oxidized and decarboxylated to alpha-ketoglutarate (5 carbons), producing NADH and releasing CO2.
4. Alpha-ketoglutarate is oxidized and decarboxylated to succinyl-CoA (4 carbons), producing NADH and releasing CO2.
5. Succinyl-CoA is converted to succinate, producing one GTP (or ATP, depending on cell type) by substrate-level phosphorylation.
6. Succinate is oxidized to fumarate by succinate dehydrogenase, reducing FAD to FADH2. Succinate dehydrogenase is unusual because it is embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane and also forms part of the electron transport chain as Complex II.
7. Fumarate gains a water molecule and becomes malate.
8. Malate is oxidized to regenerate oxaloacetate, producing NADH.

Because oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end, the pathway is a true cycle: one oxaloacetate molecule can process many acetyl groups.

### Yield per turn and per glucose

Each turn of the cycle produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 ATP (or GTP), and 2 CO2. Since one glucose produces two acetyl-CoA molecules, the cycle turns twice per glucose, yielding 6 NADH, 2 FADH2, 2 ATP, and 4 CO2. Combined with pyruvate oxidation, all six carbons of the original glucose have now been released as CO2. The carbon dioxide we exhale is produced in these decarboxylation reactions, not from the oxygen we inhale.

Notice that the citric acid cycle itself makes very little ATP directly. Its main contribution is loading electron carriers with high-energy electrons. Oxygen is not used directly in the cycle, yet the cycle stops without oxygen because NAD+ and FAD cannot be regenerated unless the electron transport chain is running.

## Stage 4: Oxidative Phosphorylation

### The electron transport chain

The electron transport chain (ETC) is a series of protein complexes and mobile carriers embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The inner membrane is folded into cristae, which greatly increase the surface area available for these complexes. The main components are:

- **Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase)** accepts electrons from NADH.
- **Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase)** accepts electrons from FADH2.
- **Ubiquinone (coenzyme Q)**, a small lipid-soluble carrier that moves within the membrane, carries electrons from Complexes I and II to Complex III.
- **Complex III (cytochrome bc1 complex)** passes electrons to cytochrome c.
- **Cytochrome c**, a small protein on the intermembrane-space side, carries electrons to Complex IV.
- **Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase)** transfers electrons to oxygen, the final electron acceptor, which combines with hydrogen ions to form water.

As electrons move from carrier to carrier, each acceptor is more electronegative than the one before, so electrons fall to progressively lower energy levels. Complexes I, III, and IV use the released energy to pump protons (H+) from the matrix into the intermembrane space. Complex II does not pump protons, which is why FADH2 yields less ATP than NADH.

### Chemiosmosis and ATP synthase

The proton pumping creates an electrochemical gradient across the inner membrane, called the proton-motive force. The intermembrane space becomes more acidic and more positively charged than the matrix. Protons can flow back into the matrix only through a channel in an enzyme called ATP synthase.

ATP synthase works like a molecular rotary motor. As protons pass through its membrane-embedded F0 portion, part of the enzyme rotates, and this rotation changes the shape of the catalytic F1 portion that sticks into the matrix, driving the joining of ADP and inorganic phosphate into ATP. The idea that a proton gradient links electron transport to ATP synthesis is called the chemiosmotic theory, proposed by Peter Mitchell in 1961. Mitchell was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1978.

### ATP yield of oxidative phosphorylation

Modern estimates suggest that each NADH entering at Complex I supports the production of about 2.5 ATP, while each FADH2 supports about 1.5 ATP. Older textbooks used the round figures of 3 and 2. Because the numbers depend on the proton cost of transporting ATP and phosphate across the membrane, they are not whole numbers.

## Total ATP Yield

Adding everything together, the theoretical maximum yield from one glucose molecule is about 30 to 32 ATP. Older sources often cite 36 to 38 ATP. The exact total varies depending on which shuttle system moves the electrons of cytosolic NADH into the mitochondria. The glycerol-phosphate shuttle, common in skeletal muscle and brain, delivers those electrons to FAD and yields fewer ATP, while the malate-aspartate shuttle, active in liver and heart, delivers them to NAD+ inside the matrix and yields more.

About 34 percent of the energy in glucose is captured in ATP under ideal conditions; the rest is released as heat, which helps warm-blooded animals maintain body temperature.

| Glycolysis | Cytosol | 2 (net) | 2 | 0 | 0 |
| Pyruvate oxidation | Matrix | 0 | 2 | 0 | 2 |

## Anaerobic Respiration and Fermentation

### When oxygen is absent

Without oxygen, the electron transport chain stops, NADH accumulates, and NAD+ runs out. Since glycolysis needs NAD+ at the G3P oxidation step, glycolysis would halt. Cells solve this problem in two ways.

Some prokaryotes perform anaerobic respiration, which uses an electron transport chain but substitutes a different final electron acceptor for oxygen. For example, certain bacteria use sulfate, producing hydrogen sulfide, and others use nitrate.

Many other cells use fermentation, which regenerates NAD+ by passing the electrons of NADH to an organic molecule derived from pyruvate. Fermentation makes no additional ATP beyond the 2 ATP from glycolysis. Its sole purpose is to recycle NAD+ so glycolysis can keep running.

### Lactic acid fermentation

In lactic acid fermentation, the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase reduces pyruvate directly to lactate, oxidizing NADH to NAD+. No CO2 is released. Human skeletal muscle cells switch to this pathway during intense exercise when oxygen delivery cannot keep up with demand. The lactate is carried by the blood to the liver, where it can be converted back to glucose in a process known as the Cori cycle. Lactic acid fermentation by bacteria such as Lactobacillus is also used to make yogurt, sauerkraut, and kimchi.

### Alcoholic fermentation

In alcoholic fermentation, carried out by yeasts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, pyruvate is first decarboxylated to acetaldehyde, releasing CO2. Acetaldehyde is then reduced by NADH to ethanol. The CO2 released makes bread dough rise, while the ethanol is the basis of beer and wine production.

### Obligate and facultative organisms

Organisms differ in their relationship to oxygen. Obligate anaerobes, such as Clostridium species, cannot survive in the presence of oxygen. Facultative anaerobes, including yeast and E. coli, can make ATP by aerobic respiration when oxygen is present and switch to fermentation or anaerobic respiration when it is not. Obligate aerobes, including humans, require oxygen for survival at the organism level, even if some of their cells can ferment for short periods.

## Other Fuels and Regulation

Glucose is not the only fuel. Proteins are broken into amino acids, whose amino groups are removed (deamination) and excreted as urea, while the carbon skeletons enter glycolysis or the citric acid cycle at various points. Fats are broken into glycerol, which enters glycolysis, and fatty acids, which undergo beta-oxidation to produce two-carbon acetyl-CoA units. A gram of fat yields more than twice the energy of a gram of carbohydrate, roughly 9 kilocalories compared with 4.

Respiration is regulated mainly by feedback inhibition. Besides PFK-1, key control points include the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex and isocitrate dehydrogenase in the citric acid cycle, both inhibited by high NADH and ATP.

## Poisons and Uncouplers

Several toxic substances act on the electron transport chain. Cyanide and carbon monoxide bind to Complex IV and block the transfer of electrons to oxygen, halting ATP production. Rotenone, a plant-derived pesticide, blocks Complex I. Oligomycin, an antibiotic, blocks the proton channel of ATP synthase.

Uncouplers such as 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) make the inner membrane leaky to protons, so the gradient dissipates without producing ATP and the energy is released as heat. Brown adipose tissue in newborns and hibernating mammals uses a natural uncoupling protein called thermogenin (UCP1) to generate heat in exactly this way.
