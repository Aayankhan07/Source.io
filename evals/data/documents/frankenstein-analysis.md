# Frankenstein: A Critical Analysis

## 1. Introduction and Publication History

Mary Shelley's Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus is one of the founding works of both Gothic fiction and science fiction. It was first published anonymously in London on 1 January 1818, in three volumes, by the small firm of Lackington, Hughes, Harding, Mavor and Jones. Only about 500 copies were printed. Because the preface was written by Percy Bysshe Shelley, many early reviewers assumed that he was the author. Mary Shelley's name first appeared on the title page of the second edition in 1823, which was published to capitalize on a successful stage adaptation. In 1831 she issued a substantially revised one-volume edition with a new introduction, and that version became the text most widely read for more than a century.

Mary Shelley was born Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin in 1797. Her mother, Mary Wollstonecraft, was the author of A Vindication of the Rights of Woman and died from complications eleven days after Mary's birth. Her father, William Godwin, was a radical political philosopher. Mary was only eighteen when she began writing the novel and twenty when it was published. Her life before writing it had already been marked by loss: a premature daughter born to her and Percy in 1815 died within days, an experience many critics connect to the novel's obsession with creation, death, and abandoned offspring.

## 2. The Origin of the Story

The novel grew out of a famous summer in 1816, which became known as the "Year Without a Summer" because the eruption of Mount Tambora in Indonesia the previous year had filled the atmosphere with ash, causing cold weather and heavy rain across Europe. Mary, Percy Shelley, and Mary's stepsister Claire Clairmont stayed near Lord Byron at the Villa Diodati on Lake Geneva in Switzerland. Byron's physician, John Polidori, was also present. Confined indoors by storms, the group read German ghost stories, and Byron proposed that each of them write a ghost story of their own.

According to Mary's 1831 introduction, she struggled for days to find an idea. After listening to Byron and Percy discuss experiments in galvanism and whether a corpse might be reanimated, she experienced what she described as a waking dream of a student kneeling beside a creature he had assembled and brought to life. That vision became the seed of the novel. Polidori's contribution from the same contest, The Vampyre (1819), became the first influential vampire story in English prose, so the Diodati gathering produced two foundational works of modern horror.

## 3. Structure: A Nested Frame Narrative

One of the most distinctive features of Frankenstein is its structure. The story is told through three nested narrators, often compared to a set of Russian dolls or concentric circles.

### 3.1 The outer frame: Robert Walton

The novel opens and closes with letters from Robert Walton, an English explorer leading a ship toward the North Pole, to his sister Margaret Saville in England. Walton is ambitious, lonely, and eager for glory; he longs for a friend who can share his ambitions. His ship becomes trapped in ice, and the crew rescues a nearly frozen Victor Frankenstein, who is pursuing someone across the ice on a dog sledge. The letters are dated in the 1790s ("17—" in the text), placing the action in the late eighteenth century.

### 3.2 The middle frame: Victor Frankenstein

Recognizing in Walton the same dangerous ambition that ruined him, Victor tells his life story as a warning. This narrative forms most of the book. Victor describes his happy childhood in Geneva, his adopted companion Elizabeth Lavenza, his fascination with outdated alchemists such as Cornelius Agrippa and Paracelsus, his studies at the University of Ingolstadt in Bavaria, and his secret two-year project to create a living being.

### 3.3 The inner core: the creature's narrative

At the very center of the novel, the creature tells his own story to Victor in a hut on a glacier near Mont Blanc, close to the Montanvert valley. Placed at the heart of the book, this section gives the supposed monster the most eloquent voice in the novel. It occupies most of the second volume of the original three-volume edition.

### 3.4 Effects of the frame structure

The nested structure has several important effects:

- **Unreliability and perspective.** Every event reaches the reader secondhand. The creature's words are reported by Victor, whose account is in turn written down by Walton. Readers must judge the truthfulness and bias of each speaker.
- **Sympathy.** Because the creature speaks at the center, readers hear his side directly in the middle of Victor's self-justifying narrative, which complicates any simple judgment about who the real monster is.
- **Parallels.** The three narrators mirror one another. Walton and Victor both pursue knowledge at great personal cost; Victor and the creature both end up isolated and consumed by revenge.
- **A choice at the end.** Walton, having heard the story, finally agrees to his crew's demand to turn back south, suggesting that he has learned the lesson Victor failed to learn.

## 4. Plot Overview

After discovering the secret of bestowing life, Victor builds a creature roughly eight feet tall from materials gathered from charnel houses and dissecting rooms. When it opens its eyes on a dreary November night, he is horrified by its appearance and flees, abandoning it. The creature wanders alone, learns to survive in the forest, and is attacked by villagers whenever he is seen.

He hides in a hovel attached to a cottage occupied by the De Lacey family: the blind old father, his son Felix, and his daughter Agatha, later joined by Safie, Felix's beloved from Turkey. By secretly observing them, the creature learns to speak and read. He also finds three books, Milton's Paradise Lost, Plutarch's Lives, and Goethe's The Sorrows of Young Werther, and a journal from Victor's laboratory that reveals the circumstances of his creation. When he finally reveals himself, hoping for acceptance, Felix beats him and the family abandons the cottage. Rejected again, the creature turns to rage.

The creature kills Victor's young brother William and frames the family servant Justine Moritz, who is executed for the crime. Meeting Victor on the glacier, he demands a female companion, promising to withdraw with her to the wilds of South America. Victor begins the work in a remote hut in the Orkney Islands of Scotland but destroys the half-finished female, fearing the creatures might breed a hostile race. In revenge, the creature murders Victor's closest friend Henry Clerval and, on Victor's wedding night, his bride Elizabeth. Victor's father dies of grief. Victor then pursues the creature into the Arctic, where he dies aboard Walton's ship. The creature appears over Victor's body, expresses remorse, and declares that he will end his own life on a funeral pyre in the far north before vanishing into the darkness.

## 5. Major Themes

### 5.1 The dangers of unchecked ambition

The subtitle, The Modern Prometheus, refers to the Titan of Greek myth who stole fire from the gods for humanity and was punished eternally. Victor, like Prometheus, oversteps the boundaries set for humanity by seizing the power of creation. The novel does not condemn science as such; rather, it criticizes the pursuit of knowledge in isolation, without regard for consequences or for one's obligations to others. Victor works in secret, neglects his family and his own health, and never considers what he will owe the life he creates.

### 5.2 Responsibility and parenthood

Many critics read the novel as a story about failed parenthood. Victor brings a being into the world and immediately abandons it, refusing it a name, guidance, or love. The creature's later violence follows directly from his rejection. His reading of Paradise Lost sharpens the point: he compares himself to Adam, created by God, but notes that Adam was cared for by his creator, while he himself feels more like Satan, cast out and envious. The theme gains weight from Mary Shelley's own experience of losing children and of growing up without her mother.

### 5.3 Nature versus nurture and the question of monstrosity

The creature begins as gentle and benevolent. He gathers firewood for the De Laceys in secret and is moved by their kindness to one another. Only after repeated rejection based on his appearance does he become violent. This progression reflects ideas associated with Jean-Jacques Rousseau and with William Godwin, who argued that human character is shaped by circumstances and social treatment rather than fixed at birth. The novel repeatedly asks whether the real monster is the creature, whose violence is a response to cruelty, or Victor, whose selfishness sets the tragedy in motion.

### 5.4 Isolation and the need for companionship

Nearly every major character suffers from loneliness. Walton yearns for a friend at sea; Victor isolates himself first in his laboratory and later in his guilty secret; the creature, unique in the world, has no one like himself. His request for a female companion is fundamentally a plea to escape solitude, and Victor's destruction of the female condemns him to permanent isolation.

### 5.5 Nature and the sublime

The novel is filled with Romantic landscapes, including the Alps, Mont Blanc, the Rhine valley, and the Arctic ice. In keeping with the Romantic idea of the sublime, these settings inspire awe mixed with terror. Nature often has a restorative effect on Victor, calming him during periods of despair, yet it is also the stage for the novel's most extreme confrontations.

## 6. Character Analysis

**Victor Frankenstein** is brilliant, passionate, and deeply self-absorbed. He repeatedly fails to act when action could save others: he remains silent while Justine is tried and condemned, partly because he fears no one would believe him. He tends to describe himself as the greatest sufferer in his story, which invites readers to question his reliability.

**The creature** is never given a name in the novel; Victor calls him a "wretch," "fiend," and "daemon." The popular habit of calling the creature "Frankenstein" emerged later, largely through stage and film adaptations. He is intelligent and articulate, and his eloquence directly contradicts his hideous appearance, which is one of the book's central ironies.

**Elizabeth Lavenza** and **Justine Moritz** represent virtuous, self-sacrificing women who suffer for the actions of men. Some feminist critics argue that the novel examines what happens when a man tries to create life without women, removing the female role in reproduction altogether.

**Henry Clerval** acts as Victor's foil. Where Victor pursues natural science obsessively, Clerval studies languages and literature and responds to the world with warmth and imagination.

**Robert Walton** mirrors Victor's early ambition but ultimately chooses a different path, turning back to save his crew.

## 7. Historical and Scientific Context

The novel reflects scientific debates of its time. Luigi Galvani's experiments in the 1780s showed that electricity could make the legs of dead frogs twitch, and in 1803 his nephew Giovanni Aldini publicly applied electric current to the corpse of an executed criminal in London, causing the face and limbs to move. Such demonstrations suggested that electricity might be connected to the principle of life. The novel itself is deliberately vague about Victor's method, which allows readers to imagine the science for themselves.

The book also belongs to the Romantic movement, which valued emotion, individual experience, and the power of nature, while expressing anxiety about the Enlightenment faith in reason and progress. Coming soon after the French Revolution and amid the early Industrial Revolution, it can be read as a reflection on what happens when human beings remake the world, and one another, without considering the results.

## 8. Legacy

Frankenstein has been adapted countless times. Richard Brinsley Peake's 1823 play Presumption was its first stage version. The 1931 Universal Pictures film directed by James Whale, with Boris Karloff as the creature, established the familiar image of a flat-headed, bolt-necked figure who barely speaks, which differs sharply from the articulate creature of the novel. The book's central question, how creators should treat the beings and technologies they bring into existence, continues to shape debates about genetic engineering, cloning, and artificial intelligence, and the phrase "Frankenstein" has become shorthand for any creation that escapes its maker's control.
