# Plate Tectonics: How the Earth's Surface Moves

## Introduction

Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of modern geology. It explains why earthquakes and volcanoes cluster in long narrow belts, why mountain ranges form where they do, why the ocean floor is young compared with the continents, and why the shapes of continents on opposite sides of an ocean often fit together. According to the theory, the Earth's rigid outer shell is broken into a set of large and small plates that move slowly relative to one another, typically at rates of 2 to 10 centimeters per year, roughly the speed at which fingernails grow.

## The Layered Earth

The Earth can be divided into layers in two different ways: by chemical composition and by mechanical behavior.

### Compositional Layers

- The **crust** is the thin outermost layer. Oceanic crust is about 5 to 10 km thick and made mostly of dense basalt and gabbro. Continental crust averages about 35 km thick, reaches 70 km beneath high mountains, and is made largely of less dense granitic rocks.
- The **mantle** extends from the base of the crust to a depth of about 2,900 km. It is composed of silicate rocks rich in iron and magnesium, such as peridotite.
- The **core** consists of a liquid outer core and a solid inner core, both made mainly of iron and nickel. Convection in the liquid outer core generates the Earth's magnetic field.

The boundary between the crust and mantle is called the Mohorovičić discontinuity, or Moho, named after the Croatian seismologist Andrija Mohorovičić, who identified it in 1909 from a sudden increase in seismic wave speed.

### Mechanical Layers

- The **lithosphere** is the rigid outer layer, consisting of the crust plus the uppermost part of the mantle. It averages about 100 km thick but is thinner beneath mid-ocean ridges and thicker beneath old continental interiors. The tectonic plates are pieces of lithosphere.
- The **asthenosphere** lies beneath the lithosphere, roughly from 100 to 350 km depth. It is solid rock, but it is hot enough and weak enough to flow slowly over geological time, allowing the plates above it to move.

The distinction matters: plates are not just crust floating on liquid. They are slabs of rigid lithosphere riding on a mechanically weak but still solid asthenosphere.

## The Major Plates

There are seven or eight major plates, depending on how they are counted: the Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, and Indo-Australian plates (sometimes split into separate Indian and Australian plates). The Pacific Plate is the largest, covering more than 100 million square kilometers, and consists almost entirely of oceanic lithosphere. There are also many smaller plates, including the Nazca, Cocos, Caribbean, Philippine Sea, Arabian, and Juan de Fuca plates.

Most plates include both oceanic and continental lithosphere. The North American Plate, for example, carries the continent of North America and the western half of the Atlantic Ocean floor.

## Plate Boundaries

Almost all geological activity occurs at plate boundaries, which come in three basic types.

### Divergent Boundaries

At divergent boundaries, plates move apart. As they separate, hot mantle rock rises, partially melts because of the drop in pressure (a process called decompression melting), and produces basaltic magma that forms new oceanic crust.

In the oceans, divergent boundaries form mid-ocean ridges, the longest mountain chains on Earth. The global ridge system stretches about 65,000 km. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads at about 2 to 5 cm per year; the East Pacific Rise spreads much faster, at up to about 15 cm per year. Iceland is a place where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level, so the ridge can be seen on land.

On continents, divergence produces rift valleys. The East African Rift is an active example where the African continent is slowly splitting apart; if rifting continues, a new ocean basin may eventually form, as happened when the Red Sea opened between Africa and Arabia.

### Convergent Boundaries

At convergent boundaries, plates move toward each other. What happens depends on the type of lithosphere involved.

**Oceanic-continental convergence.** The denser oceanic plate sinks beneath the continental plate in a process called subduction. A deep ocean trench forms where the plate bends downward. As the sinking slab reaches depths of about 100 km, water released from its minerals lowers the melting point of the overlying mantle, generating magma that rises to form a chain of volcanoes called a continental volcanic arc. The Andes of South America, formed where the Nazca Plate subducts beneath the South American Plate, are the classic example.

**Oceanic-oceanic convergence.** One oceanic plate, usually the older and colder one, subducts beneath the other, producing a trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands called an island arc. Japan, the Aleutian Islands, and the Mariana Islands formed this way. The Mariana Trench, at nearly 11,000 meters deep at the Challenger Deep, is the deepest point in the world's oceans.

**Continental-continental collision.** Continental lithosphere is too buoyant to subduct deeply, so when two continents meet, the crust crumples, folds, and thickens into high mountain ranges. The Himalayas began rising roughly 50 million years ago when the Indian Plate collided with the Eurasian Plate, and they are still rising by a few millimeters per year. Mount Everest, at about 8,849 meters, sits on this collision zone.

### Transform Boundaries

At transform boundaries, plates slide horizontally past each other. Lithosphere is neither created nor destroyed. Most transform faults are short segments that offset mid-ocean ridges, but some cut across continents. The San Andreas Fault in California is a well-known example, marking the boundary where the Pacific Plate moves northwest relative to the North American Plate at about 3 to 5 cm per year. Transform boundaries produce frequent shallow earthquakes but little volcanic activity.

## Seafloor Spreading

Seafloor spreading is the process by which new oceanic lithosphere is created at mid-ocean ridges and moves away from them on both sides. The idea was proposed in the early 1960s by the American geologist Harry Hess, who suggested that the ocean floor acts like a conveyor belt, forming at ridges and eventually descending back into the mantle at trenches. Robert Dietz independently proposed a similar idea and coined the term "seafloor spreading."

Several lines of evidence support the process:

- **Magnetic striping.** When basalt cools at a ridge, magnetic minerals in it align with the Earth's magnetic field. Because the field reverses polarity at irregular intervals, the seafloor records alternating bands of normal and reversed magnetization. In 1963, Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews (and independently Lawrence Morley) showed that these bands are symmetric on either side of ridges, exactly as seafloor spreading predicts.
- **Age of the seafloor.** Deep-sea drilling, beginning with the Glomar Challenger in 1968, showed that ocean crust is youngest at the ridges and grows older with distance from them. The oldest oceanic crust is only about 180 to 200 million years old, far younger than the oldest continental rocks, which exceed 4 billion years.
- **Sediment thickness.** Sediments are thinnest near ridges and thicken toward the continents, consistent with older crust having had more time to accumulate debris.
- **Heat flow.** Heat flow from the ocean floor is highest at the ridges, where hot material rises.

## What Drives the Plates

The ultimate energy source for plate motion is the Earth's internal heat, from radioactive decay and heat left over from the planet's formation. Three main forces are discussed:

- **Slab pull**: the weight of a cold, dense subducting slab pulls the rest of the plate along behind it. This is generally considered the strongest driving force, which is why plates attached to long subducting slabs, such as the Pacific Plate, move fastest.
- **Ridge push**: newly formed lithosphere at a ridge sits higher than older lithosphere, so gravity causes it to slide away from the ridge crest.
- **Mantle convection**: slow circulation of heat in the mantle drags on the base of the plates and is linked to both slab pull and ridge push.

## Earthquakes

An earthquake is the sudden release of energy when rocks break or slip along a fault. Stress builds up as plates move, the rocks deform elastically, and when the strength of the fault is exceeded, the rocks snap back in what is called elastic rebound. The point underground where slip begins is the focus (or hypocenter), and the point on the surface directly above it is the epicenter.

Earthquakes generate seismic waves. Primary waves (P waves) are compressional and travel fastest, passing through solids and liquids. Secondary waves (S waves) are shear waves that travel more slowly and cannot pass through liquids; the fact that S waves do not travel through the outer core is key evidence that the outer core is liquid. Surface waves arrive last and usually cause the most damage.

Earthquake size is now usually reported on the moment magnitude scale, which replaced the older Richter scale for large events. Each whole-number increase corresponds to about 32 times more energy released. The largest recorded earthquake was the magnitude 9.5 Valdivia earthquake in Chile in 1960, at a subduction zone. Subduction zones also generate the deepest earthquakes, down to about 700 km, along inclined bands called Wadati-Benioff zones. Large undersea subduction earthquakes can displace the ocean floor and trigger tsunamis, as happened in the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan.

## Volcanoes

Most of the world's roughly 1,350 potentially active volcanoes lie along plate boundaries. About three quarters of them ring the Pacific Ocean in a horseshoe-shaped belt called the Ring of Fire, which also hosts about 90 percent of the world's earthquakes.

Volcanic style depends on magma composition. Basaltic magma at divergent boundaries and hotspots is low in silica, runny, and erupts relatively gently as lava flows, building broad shield volcanoes. Magma at subduction zones is richer in silica and dissolved gas, making it more viscous and explosive; it builds steep stratovolcanoes, such as Mount St. Helens, which erupted violently in 1980, and Mount Fuji in Japan.

### Hotspots

Some volcanoes occur far from plate boundaries, above hotspots fed by plumes of unusually hot mantle material. As a plate moves over a stationary hotspot, a chain of volcanoes forms, with the youngest above the hotspot and progressively older, extinct volcanoes farther away. The Hawaiian-Emperor seamount chain is the classic example. The Big Island of Hawaii is currently active, while islands to the northwest are older. A bend in the chain dated to about 47 million years ago records a change in the direction of Pacific Plate motion. Yellowstone in the United States is a continental hotspot.

## History of the Theory

### Continental Drift

In 1912, the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift, the idea that the continents were once joined in a single supercontinent he called Pangaea, meaning "all lands," which began breaking apart around 200 million years ago. His evidence included the jigsaw fit of South America and Africa, matching rock formations and mountain belts on separate continents, fossils of the same land-dwelling organisms such as the reptile Mesosaurus and the plant Glossopteris on continents now separated by oceans, and traces of ancient glaciers in regions that are now tropical.

Wegener's idea was largely rejected for decades, mainly because he could not explain a convincing mechanism. He suggested continents plowed through the oceanic crust, which physicists showed was impossible. He died in 1930 on an expedition in Greenland, long before his core idea was vindicated.

### The Modern Synthesis

After World War II, ocean mapping using echo sounders, magnetometers developed for submarine detection, and a worldwide network of seismometers set up partly to monitor nuclear tests transformed knowledge of the seafloor. Marie Tharp and Bruce Heezen produced maps in the 1950s revealing the rift valley running along the crest of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Paleomagnetic studies of continental rocks showed apparent polar wander paths that only made sense if continents had moved.

Combining seafloor spreading, magnetic striping, and the distribution of earthquakes, geologists in the late 1960s, including J. Tuzo Wilson, Dan McKenzie, Jason Morgan, and Xavier Le Pichon, assembled the full theory of plate tectonics. Wilson introduced the concept of transform faults in 1965 and described the cycle of ocean opening and closing now called the Wilson cycle. By the early 1970s plate tectonics had become the accepted framework of the earth sciences. Today, satellite GPS measurements track plate motions directly with millimeter precision, confirming the rates inferred from geology.

## Summary

- The lithosphere is broken into rigid plates that move over the weak asthenosphere at a few centimeters per year.
- Plates diverge at ridges and rifts, converge at subduction zones and collision belts, and slide past each other at transform faults.
- Seafloor spreading creates new ocean crust, recorded by symmetric magnetic stripes.
- Slab pull is generally the strongest force driving plate motion.
- Earthquakes and volcanoes are concentrated at plate boundaries, especially around the Pacific Ring of Fire.
- Wegener's continental drift was rejected for lack of a mechanism but was vindicated by seafloor evidence in the 1960s.
